Starlight: Brightness, Luminosity and Magnitudes

  • Open the demo
  • Astronomy (stars; Stars and Galaxies Unit 1, Days 5 and 9)
  • 20 to 40 minutes

What students should come away with

  • Luminosity L is how much light a star gives off each second; it belongs to the star. Apparent brightness b is how much of that light reaches us; it depends on the star and on its distance.
  • Light spreads over a sphere whose area grows as d², so b ∝ L/d². Twice as far, a quarter as bright. Going back from a brightness ratio to a distance ratio takes a square root.
  • Magnitudes run backward (bigger number, fainter) and multiply: 5 magnitudes are a factor of 100 exactly, 1 magnitude about 2.5.
  • Absolute magnitude M is the magnitude a star would have at 10 pc, so it measures luminosity; m − M measures distance.
  • Most stars near the Sun are dim red dwarfs, but most stars we see are more luminous than the Sun, because luminous stars can be seen in a far bigger volume: a selection effect.

Before class

Five modes, chosen above the picture. Inverse square: a bulb sends simulated photons through the first square of a pyramid; a screen at 1 to 6 units catches all of them, spread over d² squares; the graph is the light per square, 1/d², with 1/d dashed for contrast. Setups: The first square, Twice as far, Three times as far, The Sun from Jupiter (Ex17). Two stars: sliders for the luminosity and distance of stars A and B; the top shows them as you would see them, the bottom from the side on a log distance line, with the place where A would match B; Play moves A slowly away. Setups: Ex18 (4 times as luminous, 3 times as far), Ex19 (25 times as luminous, equally bright), Ex35 (a giant and a red dwarf), twins. Magnitudes: the whole sky over Ruston on a winter evening from the HYG catalog; Play lets the sky darken from magnitude 0 to 6.5; two menus compare any two rungs of the magnitude ladder in the graph. Absolute magnitude: a star on a log distance line from 1 au to 10,000 pc (drag it or use the slider); Play carries it to 10 pc. Setups: the Sun, Sirius, Deneb, Proxima Centauri, m − M = 10 (Ex21). Census: every HYG star within 10 pc, from above; Play widens the survey; the bars compare the spectral classes of the neighbors with those of the naked eye sky.

Running it in class

  1. Inverse square, Twice as far. Ask Q1, then Play.
  2. Two stars, 4 times as luminous, 3 times as far. Ask Q2. Then 25 times as luminous, equally bright: Play and stop at the match.
  3. Magnitudes, After sunset. Play. Ask Q3 with the ladder.
  4. Absolute magnitude, The Sun. Ask Q4 before Play.
  5. Absolute magnitude, m − M = 10. Ask Q5.
  6. Census, Count the neighbors, Play, then Why the sky lies. Ask Q6.

Questions

Q1. A screen twice as far from the bulb catches the same light. Over how many squares is it spread, and what fraction of the first square's light does each square get? Why does the graph not follow 1/d?

Q2. Star A is 4 times as luminous as star B but 3 times as far. Which looks brighter, and by what factor? How far would A have to be to look exactly as bright as B?

Q3. How many times brighter is Vega (magnitude 0) than a magnitude 15 star? Than a magnitude 5 star? A dark sky shows stars to magnitude 6 instead of 5: how much fainter is that, and how many more stars does the demo count?

Q4. The Sun is magnitude −26.76 from Earth. Moved to 10 pc, what magnitude is it, and could you still see it? Out to what distance could you see a star like the Sun?

Q5. A star's apparent magnitude is 10 more than its absolute magnitude. How far away is it? What if m − M = −5?

Q6. In the census, what share of the stars within 10 pc are red dwarfs, and what share are more luminous than the Sun? Among the stars you can see without a telescope, what share are more luminous than the Sun? Why are the two samples so different?

Common misconceptions

  • "Brighter means closer." A bright star may be close or very luminous; brightness alone cannot tell (the headlight problem).
  • "Twice as far is half as bright." A quarter: the light spreads over an area that grows as d².
  • "A bigger magnitude is brighter." It is fainter; and 10 magnitudes is 100 × 100 = 10,000, not 200.
  • "Equal brightness, 25 times the luminosity, so 25 times as far." 5 times: distance goes as the square root of the luminosity ratio.
  • "The stars we see are typical stars." They are the luminous few; most stars are red dwarfs too faint to see without a telescope, even the nearest one.

Physics behind the demo

  • b ∝ L/d²; m₁ − m₂ = −2.5 log₁₀(b₁/b₂) (Pogson 1856: 5 magnitudes = 100 exactly); m − M = 5 log₁₀(d/10 pc), no dust.
  • The Sun: M_V = 4.81 (Willmer 2018), m = −26.76 from 1 au; full Moon −12.74 (NASA Moon Fact Sheet); Venus at its brightest −4.92 (Mallama and Hilton 2018). Binoculars about 9 and the Hubble eXtreme Deep Field about 30 are round numbers.
  • Stars: HYG database v3.8 (D. Nash, CC BY-SA 4.0), from Hipparcos, the Yale Bright Star Catalog and the Gliese catalog: 5,070 stars to V 6 and 1,637 to V 5 in the whole sky (ratio 3.10); 324 stars within 10 pc, of which 219 are M, 50 are brighter than V 6, and 19 have a V luminosity above the Sun's. Luminosities are V band luminosities, 10^(−0.4(M_V − 4.81)) L☉. HYG is incomplete for the faintest red dwarfs and brown dwarfs within 10 pc. Sky colors from B − V (Ballesteros 2012).
  • Inverse square photons are simulated: random directions through the first square, straight lines, all caught by the screen.

Going further

  • Cosmic Distance Ladder uses m − M with Cepheids and supernovae; Parallax gives the first rung; Stellar Evolution (the H-R diagram) uses absolute magnitude for luminosity.
