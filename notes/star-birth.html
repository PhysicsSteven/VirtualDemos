Star Birth

  • Open the demo
  • Intro astronomy (GenEd, algebra); majors astronomy (virial theorem, polytropes)
  • 20 to 30 minutes

What students should come away with

  • A cloud collapses when gravity beats its pressure. Pressure is P = nkT, so cold, dense gas is easiest to squeeze. The Jeans mass is the least mass that can collapse at a given temperature and density, and it grows as T^{3/2} and shrinks as n^{−1/2}.
  • As a cloud falls in it gets denser, its Jeans mass drops, and it breaks into pieces: stars are born in clusters.
  • A core shrinking from 10,000 AU to the size of the Sun shrinks about a million times and gets about 10¹⁸ times denser. Any spin it had grows as 1/size², so gas cannot fall straight in: it settles into a disk, and jets carry spin away.
  • The protostar's core heats as it shrinks. If its mass is above about 0.08 M_sun (80 Jupiters), the core reaches fusion temperature first and it becomes a star. Below that, its electrons refuse to be squeezed further before it is hot enough: a brown dwarf that cools forever.

Before class

Students should know P = nkT in words (hotter or denser gas pushes harder), Kepler's third law, and Wien's law from Blackbody. Turn on Projector. The page has three stages: Cold and dense, Protostar and disk and Ignition. The set up card sits in the scene; it fades while the run plays. Play runs the stage; the time scale is written at the bottom of the scene.

Running it in class

  1. A cold dense core: 10 K. The readouts give a Jeans mass of 5.46 M_sun for a 10 M_sun core, so the cloud holds 1.83 Jeans masses. Ask Q1, then press Play: the radius graph bends down and the cloud falls in, in about one free fall time (0.338 Myr). Watch Pieces it can break into now climb as the density rises.
  2. The same core at 10,000 K. Same density, so the pressure is 1,000 times larger; the Jeans mass grows by 1,000^{3/2} = 31,600 to 172,600 M_sun. Press Play: the cloud spreads out instead. This is the lesson's Ex7.
  3. A giant molecular cloud. 100,000 M_sun at 20 K and 100 per cm³ holds hundreds of Jeans masses: many pieces, a cluster.
  4. Switch to Protostar and disk. A core turning once in 5 million years starts 10,000 AU across. Press Play and read Shrink factor and Density up by (shrink)³ at the end: 1.08 × 10⁶ and 1.24 × 10¹⁸ (Ex8). On the graph, the spin period (orange) falls as size² and the orbit period (green) as size^{1.5}; where they cross, the spin holds the gas in orbit: the disk edge, 25 AU from the star. The cocoon glows at 9.66 µm (Wien at 300 K), in the infrared.
  5. Once a million years, to 100 AU is the lesson's Ex9: size down 100 times, spin up 10,000 times, a turn every 100 years. Ask Q4 before showing the warning.
  6. Switch to Ignition. A star like the Sun: press Play; the core reaches the fusion line and the verdict reads "fusion on: a star". A brown dwarf, 50 Jupiters: the core temperature peaks at 1.56 million K and falls back; it never reaches the line. Right at the limit sits at 0.08 M_sun. Read Lightest star, for this fusion temperature: 0.078 M_sun = 81.7 Jupiters.

Questions

Q1. Predict before pressing Play: the cold dense core holds 1.83 Jeans masses. Will it collapse or spread out? What would you change to make it spread out without changing its mass?

Q2. Two clouds have the same density. One is at 10 K, the other at 10,000 K. How many times larger is the pressure of the hot one? Use M_J ∝ T^{3/2} to find how many times larger its Jeans mass is, and check with The same core at 10,000 K.

Q3. A cloud core 10,000 AU across collapses to a star the size of the Sun, 0.0093 AU across. By what factor does it shrink? By what factor does its density go up? Compare with the readouts at the end of the Protostar and disk run.

Q4. A core 10,000 AU across turns once a million years and collapses to 100 AU across, keeping its spin. What is its new period? Kepler's third law gives an orbit period of about 350 years at 50 AU from a Sun. Which is shorter, and what does that tell you about whether this gas can fall all the way in?

Q5. The dusty cocoon around a protostar is about 300 K. Use Wien's law (λ_peak = 2.90 × 10⁶ nm K / T) to find where its glow peaks. Why do astronomers use infrared telescopes such as JWST to watch stars form?

Q6. Jupiter has about 1/1,000 of the Sun's mass. How many Jupiters is the brown dwarf limit of 0.08 M_sun? In Ignition, raise Fusion holds the star up once its core reaches from 3 to 10 million K. Does the lightest star get heavier or lighter? Why?

Q7. In your own words: why can a brown dwarf never become a star, even if you wait a very long time?

Common misconceptions

  • "Hot gas collapses more easily because it is more energetic." Heat is pressure. Stars form in the coldest gas in the Galaxy, 10 to 20 K.
  • "A star forms from one cloud, alone." Collapse lowers the Jeans mass, so clouds fragment and stars form in clusters.
  • "Gas falls straight onto the star." Any spin grows enormously as the core shrinks, so most gas lands in a disk first. The disk is where planets form.
  • "A brown dwarf is a failed star that is still trying." Once its electrons hold it up, it stops shrinking and only cools. Waiting does not help.

Physics behind the demo

  • Cold and dense: a uniform isothermal sphere of molecular gas (μ = 2.33) whose radius follows R R̈ = 5c² − GM/R, the virial theorem with I = (3/5)MR²; it stands still exactly at the Jeans mass of Carroll and Ostlie (eq. 12.14). With T → 0 it falls in one free fall time √(3π/32Gρ). The fragments are schematic.
  • Protostar and disk: each parcel keeps its spin (ω r² fixed). The disk edge is r = (ω₀R₀²)²/(GM). Jets and the cocoon are drawn schematically.
  • Ignition: a fully convective protostar as an n = 3/2 polytrope (Lane Emden solved in the page: ξ₁ = 3.65375, ρ_c = 5.99 ρ̄). Its central pressure comes from ions as an ideal gas and from electrons whose ideal and degenerate pressures are joined in quadrature. With a 3 million K fusion threshold (Burrows and Liebert 1993) the limit comes out 0.078 M_sun; detailed models give 0.075 M_sun (Chabrier and Baraffe 2000). The lesson's "about 10 million K" is the round number for hydrogen fusion in the Sun's class of star; the lightest stars burn at lower temperatures and very high density.

Going further

  • The Sun's core today is at 15.7 million K. After ignition at the 3 million K threshold the Sun kept shrinking slowly, to about its present size, until fusion made all its light.
  • Below about 13 Jupiter masses not even deuterium fuses (Spiegel, Burrows and Milsom 2011): the usual line between brown dwarfs and planets.
