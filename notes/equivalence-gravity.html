Equivalence and Tests of Gravity

  • Open the demo
  • Astronomy (gravitation; OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 §13.7)
  • 20 to 30 minutes

What students should come away with

  • The equivalence principle: in a small closed room, nothing you do can tell standing still in gravity g from speeding up at g far from any planet. Falling freely is the same as floating with no gravity, which is why astronauts in orbit float.
  • The principle holds only in a small room. In a large one, things fall toward the center of the Earth along lines that are not parallel: that is a tide, the part of gravity that cannot be removed by changing frames, and in general relativity it is the curvature of spacetime.
  • Light crossing an accelerating box lands lower than it started, so by the equivalence principle gravity must bend light. The effect is tiny on Earth (10⁻¹⁶ m across a room) and large only near very strong gravity.
  • Starlight passing the Sun bends by 1.75″ at its edge. Treating light as a particle in Newton's gravity gives half that. The 1919 eclipse expeditions measured enough to favor Einstein; radio telescopes today agree with Einstein to 0.05%.
  • Mercury's orbit turns by 574″ a century. The other planets explain 531″; general relativity supplies the missing 43″, a puzzle that had stood since 1859.

Before class

Students should know Newton's second law, what a scale measures (the normal force), free fall with constant acceleration and the idea of an elliptical orbit. The demo has four views. Windowless room: two rooms, one on Earth and one in a rocket, each with a person on a scale and two balls to drop, with the ball heights against time below. Light in the box: a beam crossing a 3 m box, seen from inside and from outside, with the drop against acceleration below. 1919 eclipse: the stars of the Hyades around the eclipsed Sun on 1919 May 29, each shifted outward by the bending of its light, and below, the bending against distance from the Sun with the 1919 results. Mercury: the orbit with its perihelion turning, and the bar chart of what turns it.

Running it in class

  1. Earth or rocket? Before pressing Play, ask which ball lands first and what the scales read. Turn on Blind (Lab) and ask students to say which room is which (Q1).
  2. Rocket at 1.1 g, then Falling is floating (Q2).
  3. A very wide room: tides. Move Room width from 3 m to 1,000 km (Q3).
  4. Light in a rocket at g, then Light at 10¹⁵ g: press Play and compare the two pictures (Q4).
  5. 1919 eclipse: Einstein and 1919 eclipse: Newton; read the 1919 results off the graph (Q5, Q6).
  6. Radio telescopes today (Q6).
  7. Mercury's perihelion and Relativity alone (Q7, Q8).

Questions

Q1. In Earth or rocket?, how long does each ball take to fall 1.50 m, and what does each scale read for a 70 kg person, in newtons and in kilograms? With Blind on, can you tell the rooms apart from inside? What is the one thing the rocket's acceleration must be for the rooms to match?

Q2. In Rocket at 1.1 g, what does a 68 kg (150 lb) person's scale read in kilograms and pounds, and how long does the ball take to fall? In Falling is floating, what happens to the ball and the scale in both rooms? Explain why astronauts on the International Space Station float even though gravity there is about 90% as strong as on the ground.

Q3. In A very wide room: tides, how much closer do the two balls end on Earth when the room is 3 m wide, and when it is 1,000 km wide? What do they do in the rocket? Show where w·h/R comes from (similar triangles to the Earth's center), and explain why the equivalence principle is stated for a small room.

Q4. Light crosses the 3 m box in how many nanoseconds? At g, how far does the floor rise meanwhile, and so how much lower does the beam land? At what acceleration would it land half a meter lower? From inside the box the light curves down; from outside it goes straight. Which statement does the equivalence principle turn into a prediction about gravity?

Q5. In the 1919 eclipse view, what is the bending at the Sun's edge for Einstein and for Newton? The nearest bright star on the picture is how far from the Sun's center (in degrees and solar radii), and how much is its light bent? Why do the shifts have to be drawn 500 times bigger?

Q6. Sobral measured 1.98 ± 0.12″ and Príncipe 1.61 ± 0.30″ at the Sun's edge. For each, how many standard errors is it from Einstein's 1.751″ and from Newton's 0.876″? Which theory do they favor? Today's radio measurement gives γ = 0.99983 ± 0.00045: how many standard errors from Einstein's γ = 1, and from Newton's γ = 0?

Q7. In Mercury's perihelion, how much does relativity turn the perihelion each orbit, and each century (415 orbits)? Compare the observed turning with the pull of all the planets. Is the difference consistent with relativity, within the observed uncertainty of 0.65″?

Q8. In Relativity alone, how many years would relativity alone take to turn Mercury's orbit all the way round? Which planet's pull is largest, and why might Venus beat Jupiter even though Jupiter is far more massive?

Common misconceptions

  • "There is no gravity in orbit." Gravity at the height of the space station is about 90% of its value on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station fall together.
  • "The equivalence principle says gravity and acceleration are the same everywhere." Only locally. Tides, the different directions and strengths of gravity across a large region, cannot be removed by any acceleration.
  • "Light has no mass, so gravity cannot affect it." The equivalence principle says gravity must bend light; general relativity says spacetime curvature does. Newton's particle picture gives only half the bending.
  • "Eddington's 1919 result proved Einstein right beyond doubt." The 1919 plates were hard to measure and the uncertainties large; the strong confirmation came later, from radio measurements.
  • "Mercury's orbit turns only because of relativity." Almost all of the turning (531″ of 574″ a century) is the pull of the other planets; relativity is the last 7%.

Physics behind the demo

  • Room. Fall time √(2h/a); a scale reads the normal force m·a, also shown as the kilograms a bathroom scale calibrated for g = 9.81 m/s² would show. Tides: on Earth, balls a width w apart fall toward the center along lines that meet at R = 6,371.0088 km (IUGG mean radius), so after falling h they are w·h/R closer; the fall directions are drawn at their true angle. g at height z is GM/(R + z)² (GM = 3.986004 × 10¹⁴ m³/s², IAU 2015).
  • Light in the box. A beam crossing a width L takes L/c; the box speeds up at a meanwhile, so inside the beam lands ½a(L/c)² lower and leaves tilted down by aL/c². The pictures are to scale.
  • Eclipse. The Sun's place on 1919 May 29 at 13:06 UT from the low precision solar theory of Meeus (1998, ch. 25), taken to J2000; stars from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (to V = 5.5, with proper motion back to 1919). Each star is moved directly away from the Sun by ((1 + γ)/2) × 4GM☉/(c²b), b = d tan θ (1.751″ at the edge, R☉ = 695,700 km, d = 1.0139 au). The 1919 deflections at the Sun's edge are from Dyson, Eddington and Davidson (1920), Phil. Trans. A 220, 291; γ from Shapiro et al. (2004), PRL 92, 121101. The 1919 plates measured fainter stars too.
  • Mercury. General relativity turns the perihelion by 6πGM☉/(c²a(1 − e²)) each orbit with a = 0.38709927 au and e = 0.20563593 (JPL), period 87.969 days; the planetary terms and the observed rate (after the precession of the equinoxes is removed) are from Clemence (1947), Rev. Mod. Phys. 19, 361. The orbit is drawn from Kepler's equation with the turning sped up by the factor on the slider.

Going further

  • Pair with GPS Clocks and Relativity (the same principle applied to clocks), Black Holes and Lensing (light bending at its strongest) and Gravitational Waves.
  • Homework: use the equivalence principle to show that a clock at the ceiling of the rocket runs faster than one on the floor by gh/c², and compare with the Pound and Rebka experiment (22.5 m, 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁵).
