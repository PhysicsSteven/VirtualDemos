Tides

  • Open the demo
  • Intro astronomy; intro mechanics (gravity, Newton's laws)
  • 15 to 25 minutes

What students should come away with

  • Tides come from the difference between the Moon's pull at a point and its pull on Earth's center. That difference points toward the Moon on the near side, away from it on the far side, and inward around the sides.
  • The tidal acceleration falls as 1/d³ (2GMR/d³ along the Earth–Moon line), much faster than the pull itself (GM/d²).
  • The two bulges stay lined up with the Moon while Earth turns under them, so most places get two highs and two lows a day, each high about 25 minutes later than half a day after the last.
  • The Sun pulls on Earth about 179 times harder than the Moon, but its tide is only 0.46 of the Moon's. When the two line up (new and full moon) the range is largest (spring tides); at the quarters it is smallest (neap tides).

Before class

Students should know Newton's law of gravity and that an acceleration is a vector. It helps to remind them that all of Earth falls toward the Moon together; the tide is what is left over. There are two views, Pull on Earth and Ocean bulges, and in the pull view two arrow sets, Total pull and Tidal pull. In the pull view, drag the Moon or use Moon's distance; Real distance (384,400 km) restores today's value. In the ocean view, drag the Moon around its orbit or the city around Earth, and use City's local solar time and Days since new moon. Turn on Projector for the room.

Running it in class

  1. Total pull vs tidal pull. Every arrow points toward the Moon, the near side's a little longer (about 3%). Ask Q1, then click Tidal pull: subtract the center's pull and the arrows point outward on both the near and far sides and inward at the sides.
  2. Moon closer (1/d³). At 40 R_E the readouts show the pull at × 2.28 today's value but the tide at × 3.43. Drag the Moon in and out and compare the two curves on the plot.
  3. Two bulges. This opens the Ocean bulges view. The bulges sit under the Moon and opposite it; Earth turns under them.
  4. A day at the beach. Follow the city's water level on the plot: two highs and two lows a day. Read Next high tide, let it play, and note that each high arrives later each day. Ask why (Q6).
  5. Spring and neap. Now with Include the Sun's tide on. A month goes by at 48 hours per second; watch the daily range on the plot swell at new and full moon and shrink at the quarters.
  6. Sun vs Moon. Paused at first quarter, with each body's bulge outlined. The Sun's bulge (0.164 m) is at right angles to the Moon's (0.357 m). Point at the two ratios in the rail: 179× the pull, 0.460× the tide.

Questions

Q1. Predict: the Moon pulls every part of Earth toward itself. Once you subtract the pull on Earth's center, which way does the leftover (tidal) acceleration point on the side of Earth facing away from the Moon? Sketch it, then check with Tidal pull.

Q2. The Moon has mass 7.34 × 10²² kg and is 3.84 × 10⁸ m away; Earth's radius is 6.37 × 10⁶ m. Find the Moon's pull at Earth's center, GM/d², and the tidal acceleration along the Earth–Moon line, 2GMR/d³. What fraction of the pull is the tidal part? (G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².)

Q3. Suppose the Moon were at 40 Earth radii instead of today's 60.3. By what factor would its pull on Earth change? By what factor would the tides change? Check with the Moon closer (1/d³) preset.

Q4. The Sun is 2.71 × 10⁷ times as massive as the Moon and 389 times as far away. Find the ratio of the Sun's pull on Earth to the Moon's, and the ratio of their tides. Explain in a sentence how the Sun can pull harder yet raise a smaller tide.

Q5. In the equilibrium model, the height of one body's tide is h = A(3cos²θ − 1)/2, with A = 0.357 m for the Moon and 0.164 m for the Sun. A single body gives a daily range of 1.5A. What are the daily ranges at spring tide and at neap tide? How much bigger is the spring range? Check with Range today in the Spring and neap preset.

Q6. The Moon goes around Earth once every 29.53 days (relative to the Sun), in the same direction Earth spins. Show that the city comes back under the Moon every 24 h 50 min, and explain why high tides come about 12 h 25 min apart.

Q7. A student explains the far side bulge by saying "Earth's spin flings the water outward on the far side." Use the demo to argue against this explanation. (Hint: does the tidal arrow pattern in the pull view depend on Earth's rotation at all?)

Common misconceptions

  • "The Moon pulls the water toward it, so there should be one bulge." The near side is pulled more than the center, and the center more than the far side. Relative to the center, the near side water is pulled toward the Moon and the far side water is left behind: two bulges.
  • "The Sun hardly matters because it is so far away." The Sun pulls 179 times harder than the Moon. What matters for tides is the difference in pull across Earth, which falls as 1/d³, so the Sun's tide is a bit less than half the Moon's but still large enough to make spring and neap tides.
  • "Spring tides happen in spring." Spring tides happen twice a month, at new and full moon.
  • "High tide is always when the Moon is overhead." That is true only in this equilibrium model. Real oceans respond with a delay set by basins and coastlines, so local high tide can come hours after the Moon crosses the meridian, and ranges vary from centimeters to over 15 m.

Physics behind the demo

  • Pull view: the Moon's acceleration GM/r² at each point, and the tidal acceleration (that minus the pull at Earth's center). The readouts give the leading terms, 2GMR_E/d³ along the line and GMR_E/d³ inward across it, plus the exact near and far side values (near side 1.025 × the formula, far side 0.976 ×, at today's distance).
  • Ocean view: the equilibrium tide on an Earth covered by a deep ocean, h = A(3cos²θ − 1)/2 for each body, with A = (M/M_E)(R_E/d)³ R_E, added for Moon and Sun. Highs of +A under the body and opposite it, lows of −A/2 at 90°, range 1.5A for one body; with both, the range is 1.5|A_Moon e^(2iφ) + A_Sun|, where φ is the Moon's angle from the Sun.
  • The bulge's own gravity and Earth's elastic response are ignored. Moon and Sun are in Earth's equatorial plane on circular orbits. The city is on the equator. Sizes, distances and the ocean height are not to scale.
  • The lunar day is 24 h/(1 − 1/29.53) = 24 h 50 min.

Going further

  • Tidal friction: the rail notes that friction drags the bulges a little ahead of the Moon. Ask students to explain why that makes the Moon recede (about 3.8 cm per year) and the day lengthen.
  • Homework: the Moon's tide on Earth versus Earth's tide on the Moon. Using A = (M/M_body)(R_body/d)³ R_body, show that Earth's equilibrium tide on the Moon (R = 1737 km) is about 13 m, some 37 times the Moon's tide on Earth, and connect it to tidal locking.
