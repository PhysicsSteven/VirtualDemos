Life in the Universe

  • Open the demo
  • Astronomy module (Cosmology course, life in the universe); GenEd, algebra only
  • 15 to 40 minutes

What students should come away with

  • Life started early on Earth, within about a billion years, but stayed single celled for most of Earth's history. Animals arrived in the last eighth, and people in the last fraction of a second of a class period.
  • A habitable zone is the range of distances where a planet could keep liquid water on its surface. A star L times as bright as the Sun gives the same sunlight √L times farther out, so the zone scales as √L.
  • Brighter stars burn out faster: with L ≈ M⁴ the lifetime is about 10 billion years × M/L = 10/M³. A star of 2 solar masses lasts about 1.25 billion years, less than life on Earth needed to get complicated.
  • The Sun brightens as it ages, so its habitable zone moves outward; in about a billion years Earth will be too warm to keep its oceans.
  • The Drake equation multiplies a birth rate of talking civilizations by how long each one lasts. Every factor after the first is uncertain by large factors, so the answer runs from "we are alone" to millions, and the lifetime L matters most. More civilizations means closer neighbors, but only as 1/√N.

Before class

Three modes, chosen above the picture. Earth's history: Squeeze Earth's history into one 75 minute class, one day or one calendar year; Age of the Earth 4.6 billion years (the class's round value) or 4.54 (measured); Color the eons. Press Play and the hand sweeps from Earth's birth to now; the readouts give each event's place on the clock. Setups: Earth's history in one class, Earth's history in one year. Habitable zones: Star (the Sun, stars of 2, 1.5 and 0.8 solar masses, Proxima Centauri, TRAPPIST-1, or Your own star with Star's mass M); Planet's distance d; The Sun brightens as it ages. The graph under the picture shows the zone's edges against the star's age, up to the end of its life. Setups: The Sun's habitable zone over time, A bright star dies young, A good bet: 0.8 solar masses, Proxima b, close in, TRAPPIST-1's seven planets. Drake equation: a slider for each factor, R* to L, on log scales over each factor's range; the galaxy below fills with simulated civilizations, each living L years, and their radio signals spread at one light year a year. Setups: Class example, L = 1,000 years, Class example, L = 1 million years, An optimist's guess, Rare Earth.

Running it in class

  1. Earth's history in one class. Ask Q1 before Play, then play it and stop at each event.
  2. The Sun's habitable zone over time. Play; the zone creeps past Earth. Ask Q2.
  3. A bright star dies young, then A good bet: 0.8 solar masses. Ask Q3.
  4. Proxima b, close in. Ask Q4.
  5. Class example, L = 1,000 years, then Class example, L = 1 million years. Play each. Ask Q5 and Q6.

Questions

Q1. Squeeze Earth's 4.6 billion years into a 75 minute class. Life appeared about 3.5 billion years ago and the dinosaurs died 66 million years ago. At what minute of class does each happen? How many seconds before the end do the first humans (300,000 years ago) appear?

Q2. The Sun's habitable zone runs from 0.95 to 1.4 au. A star 16 times as bright as the Sun has its zone where? A star a quarter as bright?

Q3. Using L = M⁴ and a lifetime of 10 billion years × M/L, how long do stars of 2, 1.5 and 0.8 solar masses live? Which of them would you search for complex life, and why?

Q4. Proxima Centauri gives off 0.00155 of the Sun's light. Where is its habitable zone? Its planet Proxima b orbits at 0.049 au. Is it in the zone, and what else might make life hard there?

Q5. With R* = 3 a year, fp = 1, ne = 0.1, fl = 1, fi = 0.01 and fc = 0.5, how many new talking civilizations appear each year? What is N for L = 1,000 years and for L = 1 million years?

Q6. If N civilizations are spread evenly over the Milky Way's disk, 100,000 light years across, each has an area of π × 50,000² ÷ N to itself. How far apart are neighbors for N = 100 and for N = 10,000? How long would a radio "hello" and its answer take for each?

Common misconceptions

  • "Life on Earth is mostly recent." Microbes appeared early; it is complex life that is recent.
  • "A brighter, hotter star is better for life." It burns out in a few hundred million to a few billion years.
  • "The Drake equation gives an answer." It organizes our ignorance: each uncertain factor multiplies the uncertainty.
  • "A thousand times more civilizations would put them a thousand times closer." In a flat disk the spacing goes as 1/√N, so only about 32 times closer.

Physics behind the demo

Event times are cited on the page (Earth's age 4.54 billion years, Dalrymple 2001; the class rounds it to 4.6; eon boundaries from the International Chronostratigraphic Chart). On a clock of length C, an event t years ago at Earth age T sits at C(1 − t/T). The habitable zone is 0.95 to 1.37 au for the Sun (Kasting, Whitmire and Reynolds 1993; the class rounds the outer edge to 1.4) scaled by √L by the inverse square law. Main sequence stars use the class rules L ≈ M⁴ and t ≈ 10 billion years × M/L; Proxima and TRAPPIST-1 use their measured masses and luminosities. The Sun's brightening follows Gough (1981), L(t) = 1/(1 + 0.4(1 − t/4.57 Gyr)). The no greenhouse temperature is T = 255 K × L^¼/√d for Earth's reflectivity 0.30. The galaxy simulation draws births as a Poisson process at R* fp ne fl fi fc a year, each living L years, so the count settles near N and scatters by about √N; past 20,000 civilizations each dot stands for a power of ten of them. The galaxy picture and the positions of the civilizations are simulated.

Going further

  • Switch the Age of the Earth to 4.54 billion years: how much do the clock times change?
  • TRAPPIST-1: with the simple √L rule, which planets are in the zone? Models that include the planets' air put e, f and g in it. Why might the air matter?
  • Find the smallest L for which N stays above 1 with the class example's other factors.
