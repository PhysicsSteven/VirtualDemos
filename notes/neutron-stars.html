Neutron Stars and Pulsars

  • Open the demo
  • Intro astronomy (GenEd, algebra); majors astronomy (rotational energy, magnetic dipole braking)
  • 20 to 30 minutes

What students should come away with

  • A neutron star packs more than a solar mass into a ball about 20 km across, as dense as an atomic nucleus: about 5 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³.
  • A collapsing core keeps its spin and its magnetic flux. Shrink it by a factor f and it spins f² times faster and its field grows f² times stronger.
  • A pulsar is a spinning neutron star whose beams, along its magnetic axis, sweep past Earth like a lighthouse. The time between pulses is the spin period. If neither beam crosses our line of sight we see no pulses, though the star is still there.
  • Pulsars slow down as they lose spin energy. The Crab pulsar's lost spin energy, about 100,000 Suns, is what keeps the Crab Nebula glowing.

Before class

Students should know the density formula ρ = M/V and the idea that a spinning skater speeds up when she pulls her arms in. Turn on Projector. The three views are Collapse, Lighthouse and Spin down. Drag the scene, or use the arrow keys on it, to turn the star; the time scale is written in the scene.

Running it in class

  1. A core 2,000 km in radius turning once a day is the lesson's Ex27. Ask Q2 first, then press Play. The radius is drawn on a log scale. At the end: shrink factor 200, spin 40,000 times faster, P = 86,400 s / 40,000 = 2.16 s, and the field 40,000 times stronger. The graph shows P falling as R² and B rising as 1/R².
  2. If the Sun collapsed squeezes the whole Sun to 10 km: it would need to spin faster than a ball that size can hold together. Real cores lose most of their spin on the way in.
  3. Heaviest neutron star known: PSR J0740+6620, 2.08 M_sun in about 12.4 km. Read the density and compare with an atomic nucleus.
  4. Switch to Lighthouse. Bell's first pulsar: one pulse a turn, shown in real time: a pulse every 1.337 s on the radio trace below. Both beams cross us: two pulses gives a main pulse and an interpulse, as the Crab shows. The beams miss us: no pulses at all. Tilt the beams with the sliders and watch Pulses each turn.
  5. Switch to Spin down. The Crab pulsar: P = 33.4 ms, slowing by 36.4 ns a day. Its characteristic age P/2Ṗ is 1,257 years; the supernova was seen in 1054. Read the energy lost: 4.5 × 10³¹ W, about 117,000 Suns. Bell's first pulsar, slowing is an old pulsar: about 16 million years by P/2Ṗ, with a weaker field.

Questions

Q1. A neutron star has 2.0 M_sun (M_sun = 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg) and a radius of 12 km. Find its density. How does it compare with an atomic nucleus, about 2.7 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³?

Q2. A star's core of radius 2,000 km turns once a day. It collapses to a neutron star of radius 10 km, keeping its spin. What is the new period? Check with Play.

Q3. A core of radius 1,000 km turning once every 12 hours collapses to 10 km. What is the new period? If its magnetic field was 1,000 T, what is it after the collapse?

Q4. The Crab pulsar spins about 30 times a second and has a radius of about 10 km. What is its period, and how fast does its equator move? Compare with the speed of light.

Q5. Pulsar pulses are perfectly regular. What sets the time between pulses? In Lighthouse, why do some setups give two pulses each turn, and The beams miss us none?

Q6. Astronomers know of a few thousand pulsars. Use The beams miss us to explain why there must be many more neutron stars than pulsars we can see.

Q7. The Crab pulsar slows by 36 ns every day. About how many years until its period has doubled from 33 ms to 67 ms, if it kept slowing at today's rate? (The real slowing gets gentler as it slows.) Compare with its characteristic age τ = P/2Ṗ.

Common misconceptions

  • "Pulsars pulse: the star brightens and dims." The beams are steady; they sweep past us as the star turns.
  • "Every neutron star is a pulsar." Only those whose beams cross our line of sight, and only while they spin fast enough to make beams.
  • "The collapse makes the star spin, as if something pushes it." It already spun slowly. Keeping its angular momentum while shrinking makes it spin faster.
  • "A neutron star is a big atom." It is held up by neutron degeneracy pressure and the strong force, and it is gravitationally bound, not bound like a nucleus.

Physics behind the demo

  • Collapse: the core keeps its angular momentum and shape (I = kMR², k fixed), so P ∝ R², and its magnetic flux BπR², so B ∝ 1/R². Real cores are not uniform and shed spin and field; the collapse takes a fraction of a second.
  • Lighthouse: the pulse geometry is exact for a beam of half width ρ around each magnetic pole: cos θ = cos α cos ζ + sin α sin ζ cos φ, a pulse while θ < ρ (Lorimer and Kramer 2005). The beam shape across its width is schematic.
  • Spin down: magnetic dipole braking in vacuum with I = 10³⁸ kg m² and R = 10 km, as the ATNF Pulsar Catalogue assumes: Ė = 4π²IṖ/P³, τ = P/2Ṗ, B = 3.2 × 10¹⁵ √(PṖ) T. Real pulsars brake with indices below 3.
  • Periods and their rates of change are from the ATNF Pulsar Catalogue.

Going further

  • Jocelyn Bell Burnell found the first pulsar in 1967 as a regular bit of "scruff" on a chart recorder; the period was so regular the team nicknamed it LGM-1.
  • The fastest known pulsars spin more than 700 times a second: they were spun up by gas falling on from a companion star.
