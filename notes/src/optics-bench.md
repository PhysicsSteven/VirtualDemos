## What students should come away with

- An image is where the rays from one object point cross, or where they appear to come from when traced back. Two of the three principal rays are enough to find it.
- The thin lens and mirror equation 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f and m = −d_i/d_o, with signs: d_i > 0 is a real image, m < 0 is inverted, f < 0 for a diverging lens.
- A converging lens or concave mirror gives a real, inverted image of an object beyond F and a virtual, upright, enlarged one inside F. A diverging lens always gives a virtual, upright, reduced image.
- Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Light inside glass cannot leave past the critical angle θ_c = arcsin(1/n); and because n depends on wavelength, a prism spreads white light.
- Two converging lenses a distance f₁ + f₂ apart take a parallel beam to a parallel beam, turned through a larger angle: a telescope with angular magnification f₁/f₂.

## Before class

Students should know the law of reflection and Snell's law. The sign conventions are spelled out under the Image readouts; agree on them first. Click an element to select it: lenses show draggable F and F′ dots, mirrors F and C, and a round knob turns it. Image readouts need an **Object arrow** whose rays meet exactly one lens or mirror.

## Running it in class

1. **Converging lens.** Ask Q1 first. The readouts give d_o = +30.0 cm, d_i = +15.0 cm, m = −0.50. Drag the object toward the lens: inside 2F the image is enlarged, at F it goes to infinity, inside F it becomes virtual.
2. **Magnifier.** The object is inside F (6 cm from an f = 12 cm lens). The rays leave diverging; the dashed lines trace them back to a virtual, upright image at d_i = −12.0 cm with m = +2.00 (Q2).
3. **Diverging.** Back in **Converging lens**, click the lens and choose **Diverging**: f = −10 cm with the object still at 30 cm gives d_i = −7.5 cm, m = +0.25. Drag the object anywhere in front of the lens: the image stays virtual, upright and reduced (Q3).
4. **Concave mirror.** Traced d_i = +19.7 cm, while the mirror equation gives +20.0 cm. Ask what causes the difference, then try the fix students suggest (Q4).
5. **Telescope.** A beam at 3° to the axis enters a 16 cm lens; the 5 cm lens sits 21 cm behind it. The beam leaves parallel, narrower, tilted the other way and steeper. The **Telescope** readouts measure the angular magnification from the traced beam, θ_out/θ_in = −3.20×, beside M = −f_obj/f_eye (Q5).
6. **Prism**, then **Total internal reflection.** A single white ray splits; violet bends most. Then select the beam and turn **Angle (pivots on the glass)**: the beam enters the semicircle along a radius and meets the flat face at the angle in **Angle inside, at exit face**. Step past 41.8° and the outgoing ray disappears (Q6).
7. **Microscope.** A 2 cm objective, a 5 cm eyepiece and a 0.5 cm object 2.25 cm from the objective. The red arrow between the lenses is the objective's real, inverted image, 18.0 cm behind it (m_obj = −8.00×); it sits at the eyepiece's focal point, so the rays leave parallel and the eye is relaxed. The **Microscope** readouts give M_eye = 25 cm/f_eye = +5.00× and M_net = m_obj × M_eye = −40.00×, and the angular magnification measured from the traced rays agrees. Select the eyepiece and move it: the final image leaves infinity and the measured M disappears until it is back (Q7).
8. **Refracting surface**, then **Apparent depth.** One curved glass surface (n₁ = 1.00, n₂ = 1.50, R = +4 cm) forms an image by itself: n₁/d_o + n₂/d_i = (n₂ − n₁)/R gives +24.0 cm, but the traced rays cross at +21.4 cm. Select the surface and shrink **Aperture height**: the traced image walks out to the paraxial one. **Apparent depth** puts a fish 12 cm deep in water behind a flat surface (drawn upright, the eye on the right): it appears 9.0 cm deep, at −(n₂/n₁)d_o. Narrow the fish's fan to see why viewing at an angle makes it look shallower still (Q8).
9. **A shape, point by point.** A flat house shape 17 cm in front of an f = 8 cm lens. Every corner is traced like a single point source; the image is drawn from the traced corners. Its depth along the axis is squeezed differently from its height: the traced depth ratio matches m₁m₂ = 0.804 from the lens equation. Then **A shape in a magnifier**: the image is 2.2 times taller but 3.9 times deeper, so a magnified object looks stretched along the axis.
10. **Fermat: the quickest path.** A lamp A in air, a point B under water, and a trial path through P on the surface. Ask Q10, then drag P: the time is least at x = 1.69 cm, where n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ (0.8254 both). Tick **Show the quickest path** to check. Switch to **Off a mirror**: the quickest path makes equal angles.

## Questions

**Q1.** Predict: an object stands 30 cm in front of a converging lens with f = 10 cm. Where is the image, and is it upright or inverted, larger or smaller? Predict the same for the object at 15 cm and at 5 cm. Check by dragging the object in **Converging lens**.

**Q2.** In **Magnifier** the object is 6.0 cm from a lens with f = 12.0 cm. Find d_i and m from the lens equation. Where must the object be placed for m = +3? Check by moving it (arrow keys move a selected item by 0.5 cm).

**Q3.** Explain, using the lens equation and the principal rays, why a diverging lens can never form a real image of a real object, and why its image is always smaller than the object.

**Q4.** In **Concave mirror** (R = 24 cm, object 3 cm tall at 30 cm) the traced image is at 19.7 cm, but 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f gives 20.0 cm. Predict which change would bring the two into agreement: a smaller mirror aperture, a narrower fan of rays, or a shorter object. Try all three. What does the result tell you about when the mirror equation holds?

**Q5.** In **Telescope**, f₁ = 16 cm and f₂ = 5 cm. Explain why the lenses must be f₁ + f₂ apart for a parallel beam to leave parallel. The beam enters 6 cm wide at 3° to the axis. At what angle does it leave, and how wide is it?

**Q6.** The semicircle in **Total internal reflection** has n = 1.50. Why does the beam enter the curved side without bending? Find the critical angle. At 30° inside the glass, at what angle does the beam leave the flat face? Predict what you see as the inside angle approaches θ_c, then check.

**Q7.** In **Microscope**, f_obj = 2.0 cm, f_eye = 5.0 cm and the object is 2.25 cm from the objective. Find the objective's image distance d_i, m_obj, M_eye = 25 cm/f_eye and M_net = −(d_i/d_o)(25 cm/f_eye). The tube length L (objective's image side focal point to the eyepiece's focal point) is 16 cm here: compare your M_net with the textbook estimate −(16 cm)(25 cm)/(f_obj f_eye) and explain why they agree. Then keep L = 16 cm but use a 1.0 cm objective: where must the object be, and what is M_net?

**Q8.** In **Refracting surface** (n₁ = 1.00, n₂ = 1.50, R = +4.0 cm, object 16.0 cm from the vertex), find d_i and m from n₁/d_o + n₂/d_i = (n₂ − n₁)/R and m = −n₁d_i/(n₂d_o). The traced image is at +21.4 cm. Predict what happens to it as you shrink the aperture from 3 cm to 1 cm, then check, and explain the difference. In **Apparent depth**, a fish is 12.0 cm behind a flat water surface (n = 1.33). How deep does it look from straight on? Where would it have to be to look 6.0 cm deep?

**Q9.** In **Prism**, click the prism and choose **Crown**, then **Flint**, then **Diamond**. For each, note n at 400 nm and 700 nm from the editor. Which material spreads the colors most, and why does a larger change of n with wavelength mean a wider spectrum? For crown glass the editor gives a spread of 1.57° at minimum deviation; check it from n sin(A/2) = sin((A + δ)/2) with A = 60°. Why does a diamond sparkle with color more than glass does?

**Q10.** In **Fermat: the quickest path**, light goes from A = (−10, 8) cm in air to B = (8, −8) cm in water (n = 1.333). Predict: is the straight line from A to B the quickest path? Find the time on the path through P = (4, 0), then find where P must be for the least time and show that Snell's law holds there.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Covering half a lens cuts off half the image."** Every part of the lens sends rays from every object point to the image. Shrink a lens's aperture: the image stays whole and only fewer rays form it.
- **"A real image is one you can see; a virtual image is an illusion."** Both can be seen. A real image is where the light actually converges and can be caught on a screen; a virtual one is where the light only appears to come from.
- **"Total internal reflection happens when light goes into a denser medium."** It happens only going from higher n to lower n, past θ_c.
- **"The magnifier's m = +2 is what the eye sees."** The lens equation gives the lateral size of the virtual image. How much bigger it looks depends also on where the eye puts that image (angular magnification).

## Physics behind the demo

- Rays are traced in two dimensions. Lenses are ideal thin lenses (each ray's slope changes by −h/f at height h), so traced d_i always agrees with the lens equation.
- Mirrors are true spherical arcs with exact reflection, so the traced image can differ slightly from the paraxial mirror equation.
- The **Screen** element (added in the **Converging lens** preset at the image) shows the picture the traced rays form on it: sharp at the image distance and a blurred patch elsewhere, a direct way to find a real image as in the lab.
- The **Refracting surface** element is one spherical boundary between two media, n₁ on the object's side and n₂ beyond; a curved one is the end of a long rod of n₂, with an opaque stop on its face set by **Aperture height**. Rays obey Snell's law exactly there, so the readouts compare the exact rays with the paraxial n₁/d_o + n₂/d_i = (n₂ − n₁)/R (R > 0 when the center of curvature is on the outgoing side): "Exact edge ray" is where the ray from the axial object point that meets the surface farthest from the axis crosses the axis. The error shrinks as the square of that height. A flat surface gives d_i = −(n₂/n₁)d_o, the apparent depth. No dispersion at this surface; other elements on the bench are traced as if in air.
- **Microscope** and **Telescope** readouts appear with exactly two converging lenses on one axis and an object arrow (microscope) or a parallel beam (telescope). The microscope's m_obj is measured from the objective's image traced with the objective alone; M_eye = 25 cm/f_eye assumes a relaxed eye (final image at infinity). The measured angular magnifications use tan θ: θ_object = h/(25 cm) for the object at the near point, θ_image from the rays leaving the eyepiece. For thin lenses m_obj = −(d_i − f_obj)/f_obj exactly, so −(16 cm)(25 cm)/(f_obj f_eye) is exact whenever L = 16 cm and the final image is at infinity.
- Glass faces follow Snell's law; the faint reflection at each face has a fixed brightness (no Fresnel coefficients).
- n is quoted at 550 nm and follows a Cauchy form; n = 1.50 gives 1.529 at 400 nm and 1.487 at 700 nm, more dispersion than crown glass so the spectrum is easy to see. White light is seven wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm.
- **Fermat: the quickest path** (Feynman Lectures Vol I, ch. 26) is a scene of its own. A trial path A → P → B takes t = (n₁|AP| + n₂|PB|)/c; moving P by Δx changes t by (n₁ sin θ₁ − n₂ sin θ₂)Δx/c, so the least time is where Snell's law holds, and t(x) has a single minimum. Off a mirror the same gives equal angles. Indices at 589.3 nm from the bench's Sellmeier formulas; air is taken as 1.
