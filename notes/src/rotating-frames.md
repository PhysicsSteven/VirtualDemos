## What students should come away with

- Newton's laws hold in a frame that is not accelerating. Seen from the room, a ball that slides on a frictionless turntable moves in a straight line at a steady speed: no horizontal force acts on it.
- Seen by a rider on the turning table, the same ball curves. To keep using F = ma in the turning frame the rider has to add two forces that no object exerts: the centrifugal force, mΩ²r outward, and the Coriolis force, 2mΩv′ at right angles to the ball's motion on the table.
- The Coriolis force turns moving things to the right when the frame turns counterclockwise (seen from above) and to the left when it turns clockwise. It does no work, since it is always at right angles to the motion.
- The Earth is a turntable. A pendulum keeps swinging in a plane fixed by the forces on it, and the floor turns under it at Ω sin λ about the local vertical, so the swing's plane turns once in a sidereal day divided by sin λ: 23 h 56 min at a pole, about 32 h in Paris, never on the equator, clockwise in the north and counterclockwise in the south.
- A real rolling ball on a turntable is different: friction is a real force, and the ball goes round a circle at 2/7 of the table's rate.

## Before class

Students should know Newton's second law, circular motion and centripetal acceleration v²/r = Ω²r (OpenStax Vol 1 §6.3, which ends with the Coriolis force). Choose **Turntable** or **Foucault pendulum** at the top of the picture. On the turntable the card sets **Seen from** (Both side by side, Room, Turntable), the table's speed in turns per minute (positive is counterclockwise), the ball's speed and aim, where it starts, whether a person in the room or a rider throws it, and whether it **Slides, no friction** or **Rolls**; **Force arrows** shows the forces per kilogram. **Roll the ball** starts it. For the pendulum the card sets the latitude, the wire's length, the size of the swing, how it starts, how fast time runs, and **Spin the Earth 1000 times faster** to make each swing curve visibly. **Release** starts it. **3D** shows the turntable room (with **Turntable** under Seen from, the camera rides the table) or the pendulum hall with its ring of pegs. **Blind** in **Lab** hides how fast the pendulum's plane turns.

## Running it in class

1. **Rolled across from the edge.** Ask students to sketch the path in both frames before pressing Roll the ball (Q1).
2. **A rider aims at the center.** Ask where the ball goes in the room and why the rider misses (Q2).
3. **From the center outward**, then **Let go of a ball resting on the table**, with the force arrows (Q3, Q4).
4. **Turning the other way.** Which way does the path curve now (Q5)?
5. **A rolling ball goes in circles.** Compare the friction force with the fictitious forces (Q6).
6. **Foucault, Paris 1851.** Predict the turn direction and time, then release (Q7). Try **At the North Pole**, **At the equator**, **Southern hemisphere** and **A short pendulum turns as fast**.
7. **Spin sped up: each swing curves** and **Pushed from the middle: a rosette** (Q8).

## Questions

**Q1.** A ball slides without friction from the near edge of a turntable 4 m across, turning counterclockwise at 10 turns a minute, straight across at 2 m/s, thrown by someone standing in the room. Describe its path in the room and as a rider sees it. How long does it take to cross, and how far has the table turned?

**Q2.** A rider at the edge (1.76 m from the center) aims straight at the center and throws at 2 m/s measured on the table. How fast is the rider moving in the room, and in what direction does the ball really leave? How close does it pass to the center?

**Q3.** A ball is sent straight out from the center at 1.5 m/s. In the room it moves along a radius. Why does it curve on the table, and which way? Read the Coriolis and centrifugal forces per kilogram when it reaches the edge and check them against 2Ωv′ and Ω²r′.

**Q4.** A ball resting on the table halfway out (1 m) is let go, with no friction to hold it. What does it do in the room? Seen by the rider it first moves straight outward: which force starts it, and why does the path then bend?

**Q5.** Turn the table clockwise and repeat Q1. Which way does the path curve now? Relate this to the Earth seen from above the North Pole and above the South Pole.

**Q6.** A solid ball rolls without slipping from the center at 0.5 m/s on a table turning at 20 turns a minute. Show from the readouts that it goes round a circle in the room at 2Ω/7, and find the circle's radius. Is the friction force a real force or a fictitious one?

**Q7.** Predict which way and how fast the plane of Foucault's pendulum turned in Paris (48.846° N). How long does a full turn take there, at the North Pole, on the equator and in Sydney (33.87° S)? Does the length of the wire matter?

**Q8.** With the Earth's spin sped up, compare the path of a bob let go from rest at the side with one pushed from the middle. Why does each swing curve to the right in the northern hemisphere?

## Common misconceptions

- **"The centrifugal force throws things outward on a merry go round."** In the room nothing pushes the ball outward: let go, it simply keeps moving in a straight line, which carries it away from the center. The centrifugal force appears only when you describe the motion from the turning frame.
- **"The Coriolis force is a real force from the spinning."** No object exerts it; it is the price of using F = ma in a turning frame, like the push you feel in an accelerating car.
- **"The Foucault pendulum's plane turns because something twists it."** The plane is set by the forces on the bob; the floor turns under it.
- **"The pendulum turns once a day everywhere."** Only at the poles; elsewhere once in a sidereal day divided by sin λ, and on the equator not at all.
- **"Water drains the other way in the southern hemisphere."** The Coriolis acceleration in a sink, about 10⁻⁴ m/s² for water moving at 1 m/s, is far smaller than the effect of the sink's shape and how the water was poured.

## Physics behind the demo

- Turntable: radius 2 m, steady Ω. A sliding ball moves in a straight line in the room. Seen from the table, r′ = R(−Ωt) r and v′ = R(−Ωt)(v − Ω × r), and a′ = −2Ω × v′ − Ω × (Ω × r′). A rider's throw adds the rider's velocity Ω × r.
- A uniform solid ball (I = 2mR²/5) rolling without slipping, no rolling resistance: from the no slip condition and the torque of friction, dv/dt = (2/7) Ω ẑ × v, so in the room it goes round a circle at 2Ω/7 (Weltner 1979; Romer 1981). A ball rolling but at rest in the room stays put while the table turns under it.
- Foucault pendulum, small swings: a two dimensional oscillator ω₀ = √(g/L), g = 9.81 m/s², with the horizontal Coriolis term 2Ω sin λ (v_north, −v_east), solved exactly. Ω = 7.2921151 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s (IERS), so the sidereal day is 86 164.099 s. The swing turns at 15.04° sin λ per hour: 11.32° per hour in Paris, one turn in 31.79 h; 23.93 h at a pole; 42.95 h in Sydney.
- Left out (stated on the page): air drag, the vertical Coriolis component, centrifugal force (inside the measured g), Airy's precession of a slightly elliptical swing. Sped up spin is labelled on the picture.
- Tests (curriculum/validation/test_rotating-frames.cjs): the turning frame path against an RK4 integration of a′ = −2Ω × v′ + Ω²r′; the rolling ball's spin integrated to confirm no slip; edge times; the Foucault solution against RK4; the plane's angle at each farthest out point equal to −Ω sin λ t; the turn times.

## Going further

- Circular Motion: the real forces that keep things going round.
- Gyroscope: another rotating system where the axis turns slowly (precession).
- On the Earth the Coriolis force turns winds into the spiral of hurricanes (counterclockwise in the north) and deflects long range projectiles by tens of meters.
