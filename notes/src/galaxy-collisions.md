## What students should come away with

- Galaxies collide often because they are close together for their size: neighbors are only tens of galaxy diameters apart. Stars are not: they are tens of millions of their own diameters apart, so when galaxies collide the stars almost never hit.
- Tides do the damage. Gravity pulls harder on the near side of a galaxy than on its far side, so a close pass draws the near side out into a bridge and flings the far side out into a long tail (Toomre and Toomre 1972).
- How a disk turns matters: a disk that turns the same way as the orbit (prograde) rides along with the other galaxy and is torn into long tails; one that turns the other way (retrograde) barely notices.
- Andromeda is falling toward the Milky Way at about 110 km/s and will make its first close pass in about 4 billion years. Whether and when they merge depends on its sideways speed, which is hard to measure.
- Real collisions end in mergers because the galaxies' dark halos soak up the orbital energy, and the colliding gas makes bursts of new stars. This model leaves both out, and says so.

## Before class

Students should know Newton's law of gravity (the inverse square) and what a disk galaxy looks like. The demo has two views. **Collision**: two disk galaxies, each a softened point mass (a Plummer sphere) carrying 1,200 test stars on circular orbits, on a parabolic orbit with a chosen closest approach; seen from above, tilted or edge on, and turned by dragging sideways. The graph plots the distance between the centers. A mode for the Milky Way and Andromeda uses their measured distance and speeds. **Why stars miss**: a scale model, with the Sun shrunk to a grain of sand, on a ruler where each step is ten times the last, and the chance that one star hits another. Everything in the Collision view is simulated.

## Running it in class

1. **Tails: two equal disks.** Press Play. Read the closest approach and when it came (Q1). Let it run and look from Tilted and Edge on.
2. **Spinning the other way** (Q2).
3. **Bridge to a small companion** and **A dwarf torn into a stream**: read the pull on the near side ÷ far side (Q3).
4. **Tilted disks (the Mice)**: drag to turn until one tail looks straight.
5. **Andromeda is coming**, then **Andromeda, faster sideways** (Q4, Q5).
6. **Stars almost never hit** (Q6, Q7).

## Questions

**Q1.** In Tails: two equal disks, when do the galaxies come closest and how close? The orbit is a parabola; Barker's equation for two point masses of 1 × 10¹¹ M☉ each, from 125 kpc to 25 kpc, gives 869.7 million years. Why does the demo's closest approach come out 25.16 kpc rather than exactly 25?

**Q2.** Run Tails and Spinning the other way to 1,500 million years. What share of each galaxy's stars ends up in tails and bridges in each run? Why does the retrograde disk keep its shape?

**Q3.** In A dwarf torn into a stream, the dwarf (radius 3.35 kpc) comes within 18.1 kpc of the big galaxy's center. Treating the big galaxy as a point mass, how much harder does it pull on the dwarf's near side than on its far side? Then do the same for a dwarf 5,000 ly in radius, 60,000 ly from the center.

**Q4.** In Andromeda is coming, Andromeda starts 770 kpc away, approaching at 109.3 km/s with a sideways speed of 17.0 km/s. When is the first close pass, and how close? Compare with the 3.87 billion years of van der Marel et al. (2012).

**Q5.** With Gaia's sideways speed, 57 km/s, when and how close is the first pass? Why does a small sideways speed change the outcome so much? Why does the model's Milky Way and Andromeda swing apart again, when real ones are expected to merge?

**Q6.** In the scale model, the Sun is a grain of sand 1.5 mm across and stars are 5 ly apart. How far away is the next grain? How many Sun widths apart are the stars?

**Q7.** Stars 5 ly apart: what is the chance that one star hits another while crossing 1,000 ly of the other galaxy? About how many of 100 billion stars would hit one? Why do the gas clouds collide when the stars do not?

## Common misconceptions

- **"When galaxies collide, their stars smash into each other."** The stars pass by; only the gas clouds collide.
- **"The tails are thrown out by the impact."** Nothing hits anything. The tails come from the difference in gravity across each galaxy, the same tide that raises the ocean on both sides of Earth.
- **"Andromeda will hit us any day now."** Its first close pass is about 4 billion years away, and even then the Sun will very likely never come near another star.
- **"Galaxies bounce off each other."** Real ones lose orbital energy to their dark halos and merge, often into an elliptical galaxy; this model has no halos of particles and cannot show the merger.

## Physics behind the demo

- **Restricted N body** (Toomre and Toomre 1972). Each galaxy is a Plummer sphere of mass M and scale a; the two centers pull each other as points softened by √(a₁² + a₂²), so momentum and energy are kept (to about 10⁻⁸ in the leapfrog). Test stars feel both centers but not each other. Units kpc, Myr, M☉, with G = 4.4985 × 10⁻¹² kpc³ M☉⁻¹ Myr⁻².
- **Encounter setups.** M₁ = 1 × 10¹¹ M☉, a = 2 kpc, disk radius 15 kpc; the companion has mass m₂M₁, a = 2√m₂ kpc and radius 15√m₂ kpc. The parabola starts at max(5q, 4(R₁ + R₂)). Steps of 0.5 Myr.
- **Andromeda.** van der Marel et al. (2012): 770 kpc, 109.3 km/s approach, 17.0 km/s sideways (Gaia, 2019: 57 km/s); masses 1.0 and 1.5 × 10¹² M☉ as Plummer spheres of 20 and 25 kpc. Steps of 1 Myr. No dynamical friction.
- **Scale model.** 2R☉ = 1.3914 × 10⁶ km (IAU 2015 nominal). Hit chance n π (2R☉)² L with n = 1/s³.
