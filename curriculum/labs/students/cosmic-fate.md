## Goal

Find out what decides the fate of the universe: watch matter thin out while dark energy stays the same, compare four universes and their endings, and see how faint supernovae showed in 1998 that the expansion is speeding up.

## Background

**An expanding universe.** Space is stretching. The **size** of the universe a compares every distance between galaxies with its value today: a = 1 today, a = 0.5 when every distance was half as big. Light that left a galaxy when the size was a arrives stretched by the same factor, so its **redshift** z obeys

$$1 + z = \frac{1}{a}.$$

Light from z = 1 left when the universe was half its present size.

**Matter thins out; dark energy does not.** The amount of matter stays the same while the volume grows as $$a^3$$, so the density of matter goes as $$1/a^3 = (1+z)^3$$: when the universe was half as big, matter was 8 times as dense. **Dark energy** belongs to empty space itself. Every new cubic meter that the expansion makes comes with the same amount, so its density stays the same. The demo gives both densities in units of the **critical density** today,

$$\rho_c = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G},$$

the density that would just barely stop the expansion if there were nothing but matter, like a ball thrown up at exactly escape speed. Today matter is $$\Omega_m = 0.315$$ of it and dark energy $$\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685$$ (Planck 2018).

**Pulling and pushing.** Matter's gravity slows the expansion. Dark energy pushes it apart, and its push counts double (its negative pressure adds to its effect), so the expansion speeds up once dark energy is more than half of the matter density, a little before dark energy overtakes matter.

**Shape and fate.** The total of the Ω's sets the shape of space: exactly 1 is **flat**, more than 1 is **closed** (like the surface of a globe, where a triangle's angles add to more than 180°), less than 1 is **open** (like a saddle). The possible endings are a **Big Crunch** (gravity wins, the expansion stops and everything falls back together), a **Big Freeze** (it expands forever, cools and fades) and a **Big Rip** (dark energy that grows tears apart galaxies, stars and atoms).

**Supernovae.** A type Ia supernova gives off a known amount of light, so its brightness gives its distance: brightness falls as $$1/d^2$$, so a supernova that looks fainter than expected is farther away. At a given redshift the universe has stretched by the same factor $$1 + z$$; if that took a longer trip for the light, the stretching took longer, and the expansion was slower in the past than now. In 1998 two teams found distant supernovae fainter than a slowing universe allows: the expansion is speeding up (Nobel Prize, 2011).

$$G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2,\quad m_\text{H} = 1.67\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg},\quad 1\ \text{Mpc} = 3.086\times10^{19}\ \text{km}.$$

## Before the lab

1. Find the critical density for $$H_0$$ = 67 km/s/Mpc. First turn $$H_0$$ into "per second" by dividing by the number of kilometers in a megaparsec; then use $$\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$$. How many hydrogen atoms per cubic meter is that? (Divide by the mass of one atom.)
2. Light from a galaxy left when the universe was half its present size (a = 0.5). What is its redshift? How many times denser was matter then than today, and how many times denser was dark energy?
3. A supernova looks 64% as bright as it would in a universe that was slowing down. How many times farther away is it? (Brightness goes as $$1/d^2$$.)
4. Think about a universe with the same matter as ours ($$\Omega_m$$ = 0.315) and far more dark energy ($$\Omega_\Lambda$$ = 1.0), so the total is 1.315: more than the critical density, and space is closed like a globe. Will it end in a Big Crunch, expand forever, or can't you tell? Write your choice with one sentence of reasoning before you open the demo.

## Analysis
