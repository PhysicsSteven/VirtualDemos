## Goal

Measure the solar system in astronomical units, convert the distances to kilometers, and find how long light takes to cross them.

## Background

**The astronomical unit.** Distances in the solar system are huge, so astronomers measure them in astronomical units. One astronomical unit (1 au) is about Earth's average distance from the Sun, and since 2012 it is defined as exactly 149,597,870.7 km, which rounds to 1.496 × 10⁸ km (about 150 million km).

**Scientific notation.** A number like 150,000,000 is written 1.5 × 10⁸: a number between 1 and 10 times a power of ten. The power counts how many places the decimal point moves. Multiplying numbers in this form means multiplying the front numbers and adding the powers: (2 × 10³) × (3 × 10⁵) = 6 × 10⁸.

**Light travel time.** Light travels at exactly 299,792.458 km/s, about 3.00 × 10⁵ km/s. Time is distance divided by speed, so sunlight needs

1.496 × 10⁸ km ÷ 299,792.458 km/s = 499.0 s = 8.317 min

to reach Earth. A planet twice as far away gets light that left the Sun twice as long ago, so the light travel time in minutes is 8.317 times the distance in au.

**The light year.** A light year (ly) is the distance light travels in one year, 9.461 × 10¹² km, or 63,241 au.

## Procedure

**A tour of the scales.** Start at your fingertip and zoom out until you see the whole Milky Way, then come back to the solar system. On the way, watch for Earth, the Moon's orbit, Earth's orbit, Neptune's orbit, the Voyager spacecraft and the nearest stars, and note the Width of the view at each.

**The planets.** Zoom in close to the Sun, then out past Neptune. Record each planet's average distance from the Sun in au, in order from Mercury to Pluto (Pluto is a dwarf planet). Add a last row for Voyager 1, the most distant spacecraft, with its distance in 2026.

**Convert.** For each row, multiply the distance in au by 1.496 × 10⁸ km to get the distance in kilometers, and by 8.317 min to get the light travel time from the Sun in minutes.

## Analysis

1. Graph the light travel time against the distance in au. What shape is the graph? What does its slope mean, and how does it compare with 8.317 min per au?
2. How many hours does light take to reach Neptune? Voyager 1 sends its radio messages at the speed of light. About how long does a message from Voyager 1 take to reach Earth?
3. The planets are not evenly spaced. How much farther from the Sun is Neptune than Earth? How does the gap between Mars and Jupiter compare with the gap between Earth and Mars?
4. Earth is 1.2742 × 10⁷ m across and Neptune's orbit is 9.0 × 10¹² m across. How many powers of ten (how many times wider by a factor of 10) do you zoom out to go from Earth to Neptune's orbit?
5. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.25 ly away. How many au is that? How many times farther is it than Neptune?

## Uncertainty

The average distances in the labels come from the orbits, so they are exact for the model. Real distances change because the orbits are ellipses, not circles. Mars is 1.38 au from the Sun at its closest and 1.67 au at its farthest. How many minutes does the light travel time to Mars change between those two points? Is that a large change compared with your table value?

## Questions

1. When you look at the Sun (never directly!), you see it as it was a little while ago. How long ago? If the Sun suddenly went dark, how long would it be before we noticed?
2. Make a scale model in your head. If the Sun (1.39 million km across) were a grapefruit 14 cm across, how far away would Earth be, and how big would Earth be? How far away would Neptune be? Proxima Centauri?
3. A radio command to a rover on Mars takes several minutes to arrive. Why can't the rover be driven like a remote control car from Earth?
4. Astronomers use au inside the solar system and light years for the stars. Why not use kilometers for everything?
