**Using the demo.**

- A wheel spins on a light axle. One end of the axle rests in a cup on top of a post, so the wheel hangs out to the side with nothing under it. Set the spin rate (in revolutions per minute, rpm), the distance d from the post to the wheel's center and the axle's tilt, then release it.

- The wheel can be released from rest (it dips and wobbles first) or started at exactly the steady rate (no dip, no wobble). Arrows can show the angular momentum L (the spin, pointing along the axle), the torque (the twist from the wheel's weight) and the change in L.

## Goal

See how a twist (a torque) turns the axis of a spinning wheel instead of tipping it over, measure how the turning depends on the spin and on the lever arm, and use the result to understand why Earth's axis slowly circles the sky once every 26,000 years.

## Background

**Spin and angular momentum.** A spinning wheel has angular momentum, a kind of spin "amount" that points along its axle. The faster it spins, the more angular momentum it has.

**Torque.** The wheel's weight pulls down on it at a distance d from the post. A force applied at a distance from a pivot makes a twist called a torque. The farther out the force acts, the bigger the torque. A wheel that is not spinning simply tips over and falls.

**Precession.** A torque on a spinning wheel does not tip it over. It changes the direction of the angular momentum a little at a time, sideways, so the axle swings slowly around the post. A bigger torque turns the axle faster; more spin makes it turn more slowly, because there is more angular momentum to turn. For this demo the time for one turn around the post is proportional to the spin rate and inversely proportional to d.

**Earth is a spinning top.** Earth spins once a day, and its spin makes it bulge at the equator: its diameter through the equator is 43 km larger than through the poles. Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° from upright compared with its orbit. The Sun and the Moon pull on the bulge and try to twist Earth's axis upright. Because Earth spins, the axis does not stand up; it precesses instead, sweeping out a cone in space. One full turn takes about 26,000 years (25,772 years at today's rate of 50.29 arcseconds per year, from the IAU 2006 precession model).

**The pole star.** Today Earth's north pole points within 1° of Polaris, so Polaris barely moves in our sky. As the axis precesses, the pole moves slowly around a circle in the sky. About 4,800 years ago (around 2800 BC) the pole was near the star Thuban in Draco, and in about 12,000 years it will be near the bright star Vega.

## Procedure

**No spin.** Use the solid disk with the axle level. Release it with no spin and watch what happens. No reading is needed.

**Changing the spin.** Keep the solid disk with the axle level and d = 0.10 m, and start it precessing at the steady rate. Measure the time for one turn around the post at 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 5000 and 6000 rpm. Wait for the gate to show a time at each spin before you capture it. Send these readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Changing the lever arm.** At 3000 rpm, still with a steady start, measure the time for one turn at d = 0.05 m, 0.10 m and 0.20 m. Send these readings to the worksheet.

## Analysis

1. Describe what happened with no spin. Why does the wheel need spin to stay up?
2. In the spin series, what happens to the time for one turn when the spin doubles (1000 to 2000 rpm, or 3000 to 6000 rpm)? Was your guess in Before the lab 2 right?

5. At 3000 rpm with d = 0.10 m, how many times does the wheel spin during one turn around the post? (The wheel spins 3000 times per minute, which is 50 times per second.)

## Uncertainty

The gate times each turn to 0.01 s, and the model has no friction, so a repeated reading gives the same time. A real gyroscope has friction at the pivot and in the axle bearing, so its spin slowly runs down. Using your results, would a real gyroscope's turns around the post get faster or slower as it runs down? Explain.
