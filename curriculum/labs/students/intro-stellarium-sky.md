# Reading the Sky with Stellarium

## Goal

Use a planetarium program to find stars by two kinds of sky coordinates, see which stars never set from Ruston, and follow the Sun along the ecliptic through the year.

## Background

**Altitude and azimuth.** The simplest way to say where a star is: its altitude is its angle above the horizon (0° on the horizon, 90° straight overhead), and its azimuth is its compass direction (0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west). Both change all night as Earth turns, and they are different for observers in different places.

**Right ascension and declination.** These are like longitude and latitude, but fixed on the sky. Declination is the angle north (+) or south (−) of the celestial equator, the line on the sky right above Earth's equator. Right ascension is measured eastward around the sky in hours and minutes, from 0 h to 24 h. A star keeps the same right ascension and declination all night and all year (they change only very slowly, through precession), so star charts and catalogs use them.

**Polaris and your latitude.** Polaris lies within 1° of the north celestial pole, the point in the sky right above Earth's North Pole. Seen from latitude L north, the pole stands L degrees above the northern horizon. Ruston, Louisiana, is at latitude 32.5° N.

**Circumpolar stars.** As the sky turns, every star circles the pole once a day. A star close enough to the pole never dips below the horizon: it is circumpolar. From latitude L north, a star is circumpolar if its declination is more than 90° − L.

**The ecliptic.** As Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun appears to move eastward against the stars, once around the sky in a year, along a path called the ecliptic. The constellations it passes through are the constellations of the zodiac. The modern constellation boundaries were set by the International Astronomical Union in 1930.

## Before the lab

1. Predict the altitude of Polaris as seen from Ruston.
2. Using the rule in the Background, what is the smallest declination a star can have and still be circumpolar from Ruston?
3. Stars rise in the east and set in the west. In the evening, does a star in the east climb higher or sink lower as the hours pass?

## Safety

No hazards. If you go outside to compare with the real sky, stay somewhere safe, and never look at the Sun.

## Procedure

Stellarium shows the sky for the place and time you set. Clicking a star or the Sun shows its information, including its azimuth and altitude (Az/Alt) and its right ascension and declination (RA/Dec). Stellarium may give the RA/Dec for the year 2000 (J2000) and for the present date; record the J2000 values if both are shown. Turn on the constellation lines and names, and turn off the atmosphere and the ground when you need to see stars in daylight or below the horizon.

**Set up.** Set the location to Ruston, Louisiana, and the date and time to January 15, 2027, at 8:00 pm Central Standard Time (that is 2:00 UTC on January 16; if your computer is in another time zone, convert).

**Polaris.** Record Polaris's altitude, azimuth, right ascension and declination at 8:00 pm, then again at 2:00 am on January 16.

**Betelgeuse.** Record the same four numbers for Betelgeuse, the bright red star in Orion, at 8:00 pm and again at 10:00 pm on January 15.

**Circumpolar or not.** At 8:00 pm on January 15, record the same four numbers for Dubhe (in the Big Dipper), Kochab (in the Little Dipper), Capella (in Auriga) and Vega (in Lyra). Then let time run forward through a whole day and night, and note which of the four ever go below the horizon.

**The Sun through the year.** Record the Sun's constellation, altitude, azimuth and declination at 18:00 UTC on each of these dates in 2027: January 15, March 20, June 21, September 23 and December 22. That is 12:00 noon Central time in January and December, and 1:00 pm in March, June and September, when Louisiana is on daylight saving time.

## Analysis

1. Compare the altitude of Polaris with Ruston's latitude and with your prediction in Before the lab 1.
2. How much did Polaris's altitude and azimuth change in six hours? How much did Betelgeuse's change in two hours? Why is Polaris such a useful star for finding north?
3. Did the right ascension and declination of Polaris and Betelgeuse change between your two readings? Explain why star charts give right ascension and declination rather than altitude and azimuth.
4. Use the declinations you recorded and your answer to Before the lab 2 to decide which of Dubhe, Kochab, Capella and Vega should be circumpolar from Ruston. Does that agree with what you saw when time ran forward?
5. In which constellation was the Sun on each of the five dates? On which dates was the Sun on the celestial equator (declination near 0°), and on which was it farthest north and farthest south?
6. Near noon, the Sun's altitude should be about 90° − 32.5° + its declination. Check this for each of your five dates.

## Uncertainty

Stellarium computes positions from star catalogs and the motions of Earth and the Sun, accurate to a small fraction of a degree. Your readings can still differ from a classmate's if the location or the time is set a little differently. The sky turns 15° per hour. By about how many degrees can a star's azimuth change if the time is off by 4 minutes? Which of your readings would that affect, and which would it not?
