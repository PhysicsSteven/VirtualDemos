## Goal

Use the phases and sizes of Venus to test whether the planets go around Earth or around the Sun, and compare how well the world models of Ptolemy, Copernicus and Tycho Brahe match the real sky.

## Background

**Retrograde motion.** Night after night, a planet usually drifts slowly east among the stars. For a few weeks at a time, though, it stops, moves west for a while (retrograde motion), stops again and goes on east, tracing a loop or a zigzag. For Mars this happens around **opposition**, when Mars is opposite the Sun in our sky. Any model of the planets has to make these loops.

**Venus seen from Earth.** Venus's orbit is inside Earth's, 0.72 times its size. Its **elongation** is its angle from the Sun on the sky, positive when Venus is east of the Sun (an evening star) and negative when it is west (a morning star). Venus passes between Earth and the Sun at **inferior conjunction** and behind the Sun at **superior conjunction**. Like the Moon, Venus always has one half lit by the Sun; the phase we see depends on how much of that lit half faces Earth, and how big Venus looks depends on how far it is from Earth.

**World models.** Ptolemy (about 150 CE) kept Earth at rest. Each planet rides a small circle, the **epicycle**, whose center goes around Earth on a larger circle, the **deferent**. For Venus, the center of the epicycle always stays on the line from Earth to the Sun. Copernicus (1543) put the Sun at the center, with every planet, Earth included, on a circle at a steady speed. Tycho Brahe (1588) kept Earth at rest but had the other planets circle the Sun while the Sun circles Earth. All three can make retrograde loops. Kepler (1609) found that the real orbits are ellipses, slightly stretched circles, with the Sun off center; the demo's real sky (Kepler) uses them. In 1610 Galileo turned a telescope on Venus and watched its phases.

## Uncertainty

The real sky positions come from NASA JPL orbit data and are good to about 0.05° for Mars, far finer than you can read off the sky strip. Ptolemy's model in the demo was fitted to the real sky, and it is still off by up to about 1.4° for Mars. Tycho Brahe measured planet positions without a telescope to about 1 or 2 arcminutes (1/60 of a degree is one arcminute), and Kepler threw out one of his own circle models of Mars because it missed Tycho's observations by 8 arcminutes (0.13°). Could Tycho's observations tell Ptolemy's model and the real sky apart for Mars? Explain using these numbers.
