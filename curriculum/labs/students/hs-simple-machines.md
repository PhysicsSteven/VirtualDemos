## Goal

Measure how much a lever, a set of pulleys and a ramp multiply your force, find how much of your work each one wastes, and test whether a gentle ramp or a steep one wastes less.

## Background

A simple machine lets a small force do a job that needs a big one. You push or pull with the **effort** $$F_\mathrm{in}$$ through a distance $$d_\mathrm{in}$$; the machine lifts a load of weight $$F_\mathrm{out} = mg$$ through a height $$d_\mathrm{out}$$. The work you put in is $$W_\mathrm{in} = F_\mathrm{in}\,d_\mathrm{in}$$, and the useful work out is $$W_\mathrm{out} = F_\mathrm{out}\,d_\mathrm{out} = mg\,d_\mathrm{out}$$, the gain in the load's gravitational energy.

No machine gives out more work than it takes in. So a machine that makes the force smaller must make you move farther. How many times farther is fixed by the machine's shape alone, and is called the **ideal mechanical advantage**:

$$\mathrm{IMA} = \frac{d_\mathrm{in}}{d_\mathrm{out}}.$$

For a lever it is the ratio of the arms, $$L_\mathrm{in}/L_\mathrm{out}$$ (each arm measured from the pivot). For a block and tackle it is the number of rope strands $$n$$ that hold up the load, since each meter the load rises takes in one meter of every strand. For a ramp of length $$L$$ and height $$h$$ it is $$L/h = 1/\sin\theta$$. For a wheel and axle it is the ratio of the radii, $$R/r$$.

How much the machine really multiplies your force is the **actual mechanical advantage**:

$$\mathrm{AMA} = \frac{F_\mathrm{out}}{F_\mathrm{in}}.$$

With no friction, $$W_\mathrm{in} = W_\mathrm{out}$$, and then AMA = IMA. Friction turns part of your work into thermal energy, so you need a larger effort and AMA is less than IMA. The **efficiency** is the share of your work that comes out as useful work:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{W_\mathrm{out}}{W_\mathrm{in}} = \frac{\mathrm{AMA}}{\mathrm{IMA}}.$$

On a ramp, friction pushes back on the block with $$\mu$$ times the normal force $$mg\cos\theta$$, over the whole length $$L$$ of the ramp.

## Before the lab

1. A ramp 3.0 m long rises 1.0 m. What is its IMA? With no friction, what effort pushes a 10 kg crate (weight 98.1 N) up it?
2. A block and tackle with 4 strands lifts a 98.1 N load by 1.0 m. How far does your end of the rope move? With no friction, what is the effort? Show that the work in equals the work out.
3. A machine with IMA = 4 is 80 % efficient. What is its AMA, and what effort does it need for a 98.1 N load?
4. Think about pushing a crate up a ramp with friction, first a gentle one at 10° and then a steep one at 40°, both 3.0 m long. The steep ramp needs a bigger push. Will its efficiency be higher than the gentle ramp's, lower, or about the same? Write your guess and the reason for it before you open the demo.

## Procedure

The link at the top opens the demo on its Simple machines setup. In the Lab fold, tick **Blind: hide predictions and answers**: the demo then hides IMA, AMA and the efficiency, and you work them out. Every reading below uses **Block mass m** = 10.0 kg (the knob on the panel in the picture; click its readout to type a value). For each reading, set the knobs, press **Play**, wait until the load reaches the top (the status line says "At the top"), then press **Record reading** under Readings.

**Part 1: a lever.** Press **Lever** above the picture. Set **Effort arm** to 1.20 m and **Load arm** to 0.30 m and record a reading. Then set Load arm to 0.60 m and record another. Press **Export to worksheet**, then **Clear** under Readings: the two lever readings fill the Data table.

**Part 2: pulleys.** Press **Pulleys**. Set **Friction μ** to 0.20. Set **Strands n** to 1 and record a reading, then do the same for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Press **Export to worksheet**, then **Clear**: the six pulley readings go to a second copy of the Data table, and its graph is the one for Analysis 2.

**Part 3: a ramp.** Press **Ramp**. Keep Friction μ at 0.20. Set **Ramp angle θ** to 10° and record a reading, then do the same at 20°, 30°, 40° and 50°. Finally set Friction μ to 0 and record one more reading at 50°. Press **Export to worksheet**: these six readings go to a third copy of the Data table, and its graph is the one for Analysis 4.

The Export fills the first eight columns. You fill in the last three yourself.

## Analysis

1. For the two lever readings, compare AMA with IMA. What does that tell you about the work in and the work out?
2. For the pulleys, make a graph of AMA (vertical) against IMA (horizontal), one point for each n. On a frictionless machine, where would the points lie? Describe how the gap between your points and that line changes as n grows.
3. For the pulley reading with n = 4, find $$W_\mathrm{in}$$, $$W_\mathrm{out}$$ and the thermal energy $$W_\mathrm{in} - W_\mathrm{out}$$. Then do the same for n = 1. Why does the 4 strand tackle waste more energy?
4. For the ramp, make a graph of efficiency (vertical) against the height the load rises, $$d_\mathrm{out}$$ (horizontal), for the five readings with μ = 0.20. A steeper ramp of the same length is a taller one, so the points run from 10° on the left to 50° on the right. Which angle needs the smallest effort, and which wastes the smallest share of your work?
5. For the ramp reading with μ = 0 at 50°, compare the effort with $$mg\sin\theta$$, and its AMA with IMA.

## Uncertainty

The demo gives the effort to 0.01 N and the distances to 0.001 m, so your AMA and IMA are good to about three significant figures. Ask whether a difference between AMA and IMA is larger than that rounding before you call it friction. A real machine adds the weight of its pulleys and rope, which this model leaves out.
