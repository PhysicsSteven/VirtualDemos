## Goal

Find out whether the seasons come from Earth's changing distance to the Sun or from the tilt of its axis.

## Background

**Distance.** Earth's orbit is not quite a circle. Earth is about 0.983 AU from the Sun in early January and about 1.017 AU in early July (1 AU, the astronomical unit, is Earth's average distance from the Sun, about 150 million km). Sunlight spreads out as it travels, so its strength falls off as the square of the distance: twice as far means one quarter as much light on each square meter.

**Tilt.** Earth's axis is tilted 23.44° from upright compared with its orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space (toward Polaris) all year. In June the northern half of Earth leans toward the Sun; in December it leans away. Seen from the ground, the noon Sun climbs higher in the sky in June and stays lower in December.

**Why height matters.** A beam of sunlight that comes straight down lands on a small patch of ground. The same beam coming in at a low angle spreads over a larger patch, so each square meter gets less. A high Sun also means a longer day.

## Before the lab

1. Make a guess before you measure: is Earth closer to the Sun during the northern summer or the northern winter?
2. The noon Sun is highest in June and lowest in December. If the tilt is 23.44°, by how many degrees should the noon Sun's height change from June to December? (Hint: the Sun goes from 23.44° on one side of the equator to 23.44° on the other.)
3. Earth's distance from the Sun runs from 0.983 AU to 1.017 AU. Using the inverse square law, by what factor is the sunlight stronger at 0.983 AU than at 1.017 AU?

## Uncertainty

The demo computes its readouts from the geometry of the orbit and the tilt, so a repeated reading gives exactly the same numbers. The uncertainty is in what the model leaves out. Name one thing about the real Earth (its air, clouds, oceans or the size of the Sun's disk) that the demo does not include, and say whether it would make the real day longer or shorter, or the sunlight at the ground stronger or weaker.

## Questions

1. Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. If distance caused the seasons, what season would Australia have in January? What does it actually have, and what does that tell you?
2. Use your readings at 30° S and 32.5° N on June 21 to explain why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons.
3. The longest day at Ruston is in June, but the hottest weather usually comes in July and August. The demo shows sunlight, not temperature. What could explain the delay?
4. Shine a flashlight straight down on a sheet of paper, then tilt the paper. Use what happens to the bright spot to explain why a low Sun warms the ground less than a high Sun.
