## Goal

Find out whether the seasons come from Earth's changing distance to the Sun or from the tilt of its axis.

## Background

**Distance.** Earth's orbit is not quite a circle. Earth is about 0.983 AU from the Sun in early January and about 1.017 AU in early July (1 AU, the astronomical unit, is Earth's average distance from the Sun, about 150 million km). Sunlight spreads out as it travels, so its strength falls off as the square of the distance: twice as far means one quarter as much light on each square meter.

**Tilt.** Earth's axis is tilted 23.44° from upright compared with its orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space (toward Polaris) all year. In June the northern half of Earth leans toward the Sun; in December it leans away. Seen from the ground, the noon Sun climbs higher in the sky in June and stays lower in December.

**Why height matters.** A beam of sunlight that comes straight down lands on a small patch of ground. The same beam coming in at a low angle spreads over a larger patch, so each square meter gets less. A high Sun also means a longer day.

## Before the lab

1. Make a guess before you measure: is Earth closer to the Sun during the northern summer or the northern winter? Then a second guess: Earth is closest to the Sun on one of these two dates and farthest from it on the other, January 3 and July 4. On which date does Ruston get more daily sunlight: January 3, July 4, or about the same on both? Give a reason for each guess.
2. The noon Sun is highest in June and lowest in December. If the tilt is 23.44°, by how many degrees should the noon Sun's height change from June to December? (Hint: the Sun goes from 23.44° on one side of the equator to 23.44° on the other.)
3. Earth's distance from the Sun runs from 0.983 AU to 1.017 AU. Using the inverse square law, by what factor is the sunlight stronger at 0.983 AU than at 1.017 AU?

## Procedure

**The year at Ruston.** Press Pause. Leave Observer latitude at 32.5° N and Axial tilt ε at 23.44° (where the demo opens). Set the Date slider to each of these six dates and press Record reading each time: January 3 (day 3), March 21 (day 80), June 21 (day 172), July 4 (day 185), September 21 (day 264) and December 21 (day 355). The Date readout shows the date and day number; drag close, then use the arrow keys to move one day at a time. Press Export to worksheet, then Clear.

**Distance alone.** Choose the setup No tilt, which sets Axial tilt ε to 0° and keeps 32.5° N. Set the Date slider to the same six dates and press Record reading each time, then press Export to worksheet and Clear.

**Around the world on June 21.** Choose the setup June solstice (it puts the tilt back to 23.44°). Without changing the date, set the Observer latitude slider to 70° S, 30° S, 0° (the equator), 32.5° N and 70° N, pressing Record reading at each, then press Export to worksheet.

## Analysis

1. On which date was Earth closest to the Sun, and on which was it farthest? Was your guess in Before the lab 1 right?
2. For the year at Ruston, how many degrees higher is the noon Sun on June 21 than on December 21? Compare with your answer to Before the lab 2.
3. How many more hours of daylight does Ruston get on June 21 than on December 21?
4. For the year at Ruston, divide the daily sunlight on June 21 by the daily sunlight on December 21. How many times more sunlight does Ruston get in June?
5. For the distance alone series, divide the daily sunlight on January 3 by the daily sunlight on July 4. Compare with your answer to Before the lab 3.
6. Compare your ratios from Analysis 4 and 5. Which effect is bigger, the tilt or the distance? By about how many times?
7. On June 21, where on Earth is the noon Sun highest, of the five places you measured? Which places have 24 hours of daylight, and which have none?

## Questions

1. Look at your January 3 and July 4 readings at Ruston. On which date was Earth closer to the Sun, and on which did Ruston get more daily sunlight? How did your second guess in Before the lab 1 do? Use the tilt and Why height matters to explain the difference. If distance caused the seasons, what season would Australia have in January? What does it actually have, and what does that tell you?
2. Use your readings at 30° S and 32.5° N on June 21 to explain why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons.
3. The longest day at Ruston is in June, but the hottest weather usually comes in July and August. The demo shows sunlight, not temperature. What could explain the delay?
4. Shine a flashlight straight down on a sheet of paper, then tilt the paper. Use what happens to the bright spot to explain why a low Sun warms the ground less than a high Sun.
