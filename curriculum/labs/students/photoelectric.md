# Photoelectric Effect — Measuring Planck's Constant
**Student edition · 75–90 minutes · calculus-based introductory physics**  
[Open simulation](https://physicssteven.github.io/VirtualDemos/demos/photoelectric.html)

## Research question
Can measurements of stopping potential versus photon frequency recover Planck's constant independently of light intensity?

## Objectives
Calculate photon energy, work function and stopping potential; construct a linear fit to estimate h; distinguish photon flux from photon energy; identify limits of a simplified current–voltage model.

## Prelab
Derive eV_stop=hf−φ, with V_stop the **positive magnitude** of the negative retarding collector voltage. Explain why the slope of V_stop versus f is h/e and the intercept is −φ/e. Convert a wavelength in nm to f in Hz and E in eV. Predict what happens to stopping voltage when power doubles at fixed wavelength.

## Controls and apparatus
The demo has **Measure h**, **Stopping voltage**, and **UV on zinc** presets, plus wavelength, light power, metal and collector-voltage controls. The readouts include work function and stopping voltage. The **Record** functionality adds measured points with simulated noise. Record the source metal and its work function before collecting points.

## Part A — Threshold (15 min)
1. Select a metal with a low work function, such as Cs (2.14 eV). Hold power fixed.
2. Sweep wavelength from longer to shorter values until photoelectrons first appear. Record the threshold interval.
3. Calculate λ_threshold=hc/φ and compare to the observed interval.
4. Change power at a wavelength below threshold. Explain why higher photon count does not create emission in the one-photon model.

## Part B — Determine h (35 min)
1. Select **Measure h** or manually choose Cs; keep the metal fixed for the entire experiment.
2. Choose at least **eight** distinct wavelengths that are clearly above threshold (e.g. 250–500 nm where the UI allows). Record actual wavelength, calculated frequency, and stopping-potential magnitude. Avoid any wavelength that does not emit electrons.
3. For each wavelength, vary negative collector voltage until the measured current just reaches zero. If the interface gives the stopping voltage directly, record that value separately as a theory-assisted check; do not silently treat it as independently measured.
4. Use **Record** if available to collect simulated noisy measurements. For reproducibility, distinguish manually inferred V_stop from values generated by the Record feature.
5. Plot V_stop (V) against f (Hz). Fit V_stop=mf+b. Compute h_fit=e·m and φ_fit=−e·b, reporting φ in eV. Compare with accepted h and the chosen metal's displayed φ.

## Part C — Intensity versus stopping voltage (15 min)
At a fixed emitting wavelength, repeat the current–voltage measurement for three light-power settings. Compare saturation current, stopping voltage and photon rate. Make a two-column claim/evidence table distinguishing intensity effects from frequency effects.

## Model limitations
The source uses a simplified linear retarding-voltage response, I(V)=I_sat·max(0,1+V/K_max) for negative V, with K_max numerically in eV and V in volts. This is not a universal experimental photocurrent curve; its shape depends on electron energy/angle distributions and electrode geometry. The endpoint (stopping voltage) remains a useful teaching model. The built-in Record feature adds simulated random perturbations; it is not a physical instrument.

## Questions
1. What is the physical meaning of a negative fitted intercept?
2. Why does frequency change the stopping potential but power does not?
3. How do uncertainties in λ and V propagate into h?
4. What experimental effects might broaden the apparent stopping point?
5. Why would using wavelengths below threshold spoil the regression?

## Submission
Threshold estimate, eight-point data table, linear regression and residuals, h and φ with units, intensity comparison, and limitations discussion. Grading: prelab 15; data 25; regression 30; intensity study 15; analysis 15.
