## Goal

Build a simple telescope from two lenses and find out what sets its magnification and what sets how much light it gathers.

## Background

**Two lenses.** A refracting telescope has a large lens at the front, the **objective**, and a small lens near the eye, the **eyepiece**. The objective makes a small image of a distant object at its focal point, a distance $$f_1$$ (its focal length) behind it. The eyepiece, placed so that its own focal point falls on that image, works as a magnifying glass. The two lenses are then $$f_1 + f_2$$ apart and the light leaves the eyepiece parallel, so a relaxed eye sees the image sharply.

**Magnification.** The telescope makes things look bigger by the ratio of the focal lengths: magnification = $$f_1 / f_2$$. A long objective and a short eyepiece give a high magnification. The image is upside down, which does not matter in astronomy.

**Light gathering.** A star is so far away that it looks like a point, even through a telescope. What a telescope does for a star is collect more of its light. The light collected depends on the area of the objective, and area goes as the square of the diameter $$D$$: a lens twice as wide collects four times as much light. Your eye at night collects light through its pupil, about 7 mm (0.7 cm) across when it has adjusted to the dark.

## Before the lab

1. A telescope has an objective of focal length 50 cm and an eyepiece of focal length 5 cm. What is its magnification? How far apart should the two lenses be?
2. If you swap the 5 cm eyepiece for a 2.5 cm one, what happens to the magnification?
3. Telescope A has an objective 3 cm across and telescope B one 6 cm across. Which collects more light from a star, and how many times more?

## Safety

Never look at the Sun through a telescope, binoculars or a camera without a proper solar filter: the concentrated sunlight can blind you instantly. No hazards in the virtual lab.

## Procedure

**Part 1. Magnification.** Choose the Keplerian telescope, which looks at the full Moon. Set the eye's pupil to 7 mm, its size at night. Record a reading. Then change the eyepiece to a focal length of 2.5 cm, slide it so it is $$f_1 + f_2$$ = 52.5 cm behind the objective (the final image is then at infinity), put the eye at the exit pupil and record a reading. Do the same with a 4.0 cm eyepiece, 54.0 cm behind the objective. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 2. Light gathering.** Choose the Keplerian telescope again and check that the eye's pupil is still 7 mm. Keeping the 5 cm eyepiece, record a reading with objectives 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 cm across. Send the readings to the worksheet.

## Analysis

1. For each reading in Part 1, divide f₁ by f₂ and compare the result with the magnification M the demo measured. (Ignore the minus sign: it only means the view is upside down.)
2. The full Moon is 0.518° across. How big does it look through the telescope with the 2.5 cm eyepiece? The true field is the piece of sky you can see at once: does the whole Moon fit in the view with each eyepiece?
3. Graph the light from a star against D² for Part 2. Is it a straight line through the origin? What does that tell you about how the light gathered depends on the diameter?
4. Divide 1 by the slope of your graph and take the square root. Compare the result with the diameter of the eye's pupil, 0.7 cm. Why does the pupil's size appear here?
5. Using your data, how many times more light does the 6 cm objective gather than the 3 cm one? Compare with your answer to Before the lab 3.
6. The Hubble Space Telescope's mirror is 2.4 m (240 cm) across and each Keck telescope's is 10 m (1000 cm). Using (D ÷ 0.7 cm)², how many times more light than your dark adapted eye does each collect from a star?

## Uncertainty

The demo traces ideal lenses, so a repeated reading gives exactly the same numbers. Name two things a real telescope has that the demo leaves out (think about the lenses, the light and the air above the telescope), and say for each whether it would make the view sharper or blurrier.

## Questions

1. Amateur astronomers say that the diameter of a telescope matters more than its magnification. Use your results to explain why.
2. You can make a telescope magnify as much as you like by using a shorter eyepiece. What happens to the exit pupil, and to the image brightness readout, as the magnification goes up?
3. Through the telescope the Moon looks bigger but no brighter than to the bare eye, while a star looks many times brighter. What is the difference between a star and the Moon that explains this?
4. Why do astronomers build telescopes with bigger and bigger mirrors, when the stars they look at would stay points at any magnification?
