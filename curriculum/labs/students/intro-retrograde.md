## Goal

See why planets sometimes move backward (west) against the stars, measure how often it happens, and use the phases of Venus to test whether the planets go around Earth or around the Sun.

## Background

**Retrograde motion.** Night after night, a planet usually drifts slowly east among the stars. For a few weeks at a time, though, it stops, moves west for a while (retrograde motion), stops again and goes on east, tracing a loop or a zigzag. In a Sun centered picture this happens when Earth, on its faster inside track, passes an outer planet, the way a car you overtake on the highway seems to slide backward against the distant hills. Earth passes Mars at **opposition**, when Mars is opposite the Sun in our sky (elongation 180°), so it rises at sunset and is up all night.

**Position on the sky.** The demo gives a planet's position as its **ecliptic longitude** λ, the angle measured eastward along the ecliptic from the point where the Sun crosses the celestial equator in March. While a planet moves east λ grows; while it is retrograde λ shrinks.

**Synodic and sidereal periods.** The **sidereal period** $$P$$ is the time a planet takes to go once around the Sun. The **synodic period** $$S$$ is the time from one opposition to the next (or, for Mercury and Venus, from one inferior conjunction, when the planet passes between Earth and the Sun, to the next). Since Earth laps the planet like a runner on a track, the two are related by

$$\frac{1}{S} = \left| \frac{1}{P} - \frac{1}{365.26\ \text{days}} \right|.$$

**World models.** Ptolemy (about 150 CE) kept Earth at rest. Each planet rides a small circle, the **epicycle**, whose center goes around Earth on a larger circle, the **deferent**. Copernicus (1543) put the Sun at the center. Tycho Brahe (1588) kept Earth at rest but had the other planets circle the Sun while the Sun circles Earth. All three can make retrograde loops. In 1610 Galileo turned a telescope on Venus and watched its phases.

## Analysis

1. Graph the ecliptic longitude against the days since the first reading for your 14 evenly spaced readings. Where on the graph is Mars moving east, and where west? From your stationary point readings, on what dates did the retrograde motion start and stop, and how many days did it last?
2. What was Mars's elongation at opposition? Was the opposition near the middle of the retrograde motion or near one end? Using the view from above, explain in two or three sentences why Mars appears to move backward when Earth passes it.
3. On what date was Mars closest to Earth, and how does that compare with the date of opposition? Why do the two come close together?
4. Compare your measured synodic periods with the formula. Why are the synodic periods of Jupiter and Saturn only a little longer than one year, while Mars's is more than two years?
5. Graph the lit percentage against the size compared with its smallest for the real sky (Kepler) readings. When Venus looks smallest, how much of it is lit? When it looks biggest? Now look at the Ptolemy copy of the table: what is the largest lit percentage there?
6. Galileo saw Venus go through a full set of phases, from a thin crescent to nearly full, and saw it look small when nearly full. Which world model in your data can produce that? Explain why Ptolemy's arrangement, with Venus's epicycle always between Earth and the Sun, cannot.

## Uncertainty

The real sky positions come from NASA JPL orbit data and are good to about 0.05° for Mars, far finer than you can read off the sky strip. Ptolemy's model in the demo was fitted to the real sky, and it is still off by up to about 1.4° for Mars. Tycho Brahe measured planet positions without a telescope to about 1 or 2 arcminutes (1/60 of a degree is one arcminute), and Kepler threw out one of his own circle models of Mars because it missed Tycho's observations by 8 arcminutes (0.13°). Could Tycho's observations tell Ptolemy's model and the real sky apart for Mars? Explain using these numbers.

## Questions

1. Choose Copernicus's circles miss and play through the close opposition of 2020. How far off is Mars from the real sky at its worst? Why did Copernicus's simple circles not fit the sky better than Ptolemy's epicycles, even though the Sun really is at the center? (Hint: what shape is Mars's real orbit?)
2. Choose Earth held still (Tycho). Mars traces a loop around Earth. How does this loop compare with Ptolemy's epicycle? Galileo's observation of Venus ruled out Ptolemy's model. Did it rule out Tycho's? Explain.
3. Why do Mercury and Venus never appear opposite the Sun in our sky, and when do they go retrograde?
4. The loops on the sky strip are open loops or zigzags, not motion back and forth along one line. Why do the planets not retrace the same path? (Hint: are the planets' orbits exactly in the same plane as Earth's?)
5. Check the demo against a planetarium: in Stellarium (free, or online at https://stellarium-web.org), set the date to 16 January 2025 and find Mars. Which constellation is it in, and does that match the demo?
