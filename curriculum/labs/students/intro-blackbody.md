## Goal

Find out how a star's temperature sets its color and how much light each square meter of its surface gives off.

## Background

**Everything glows.** Every object gives off light because of its temperature. Cool objects, like you, glow in the infrared, which your eyes cannot see. Hotter objects glow red, then white, then blue white. Stars are hot balls of gas whose light is close to that of an ideal glowing object, a **blackbody**. Temperatures are measured in kelvins (K): 0 K is absolute zero, and the Sun's surface is about 5800 K.

**Wien's law: color.** The wavelength where a blackbody is brightest, its **peak wavelength**, is inversely proportional to its temperature. Twice as hot means a peak at half the wavelength. In numbers, peak wavelength × temperature is always about 2 900 000 nm·K. Hot stars peak in the blue or ultraviolet and look blue white; cool stars peak in the red or infrared and look orange or red.

**The Stefan and Boltzmann law: brightness.** The light given off by each square meter of surface goes as the temperature to the fourth power. Twice as hot means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 times as much light from each square meter. A star's total light output, its **luminosity**, also depends on how much surface it has.

**Spectral classes.** Astronomers sort stars by temperature into the classes O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from hottest to coolest. The Sun is a G star.

## Analysis

1. For each of your readings, multiply the peak wavelength by the temperature. Is the product the same every time? Compare with the 2 900 000 nm·K of the Background.
2. In Part 2 the temperature went up four times. Divide the peak wavelength at 3000 K by the one at 12 000 K. Is the peak four times shorter at the higher temperature, as Wien's law says?
3. In Part 2, divide the light from each square meter at 12 000 K by that at 3000 K. Compare with your answer to Before the lab 3.
4. Rigel is about 2.1 times as hot as the Sun (12 100 K ÷ 5772 K). Predict how many times more light each square meter of Rigel gives off, then check your prediction against your readings.
5. List your three stars with their colors and spectral classes. Which of them peaks in visible light, and which outside it? Where is each one's peak: ultraviolet, visible or infrared?

## Uncertainty

The demo computes an ideal blackbody, so a repeated reading gives exactly the same numbers. Real stars have dark absorption lines, and the light from their surface is not quite a blackbody's. Would a star's measured color still give a good estimate of its temperature? Explain briefly.
