## Goal

Find the five places near two orbiting bodies where a small object can keep pace with them, see why asteroids gather at two of them and why spacecraft can park, with a little help, at two others.

## Background

**Keeping pace.** A small object farther from the Sun than Earth normally takes longer than a year to go around, and one closer in takes less. But near Earth, Earth's own pull changes that. At five special places, the **Lagrange points**, the pulls of the Sun and Earth together give an object exactly the pull it needs to go around in one year, keeping the same position compared with Sun and Earth. The same is true for any pair: the Sun and Jupiter, Earth and the Moon.

**Where they are.** L1 is between the two bodies, L2 just beyond the smaller one, L3 on the far side of the big one. L4 and L5 are 60° ahead of and behind the smaller body along its orbit, each making an equal sided triangle with the two bodies.

**Stay or drift.** At L1, L2 and L3 a small nudge grows: an object slides away like a ball balanced on a hilltop. At L4 and L5, if the smaller body is light enough, a nudged object wobbles around the point and stays. Thousands of **Trojan asteroids** share Jupiter's orbit this way, gathered around Jupiter's L4 and L5.

**Parking spacecraft.** L1 and L2 of the Sun and Earth sit about 1.5 million km from Earth, always in the same place compared with Sun and Earth. The SOHO observatory watches the Sun from L1; the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) sits near L2, with Earth and the Sun always on the same side, behind its sunshield. Because they drift, these spacecraft fire small thrusters every few weeks to stay.

3. Why would JWST, which sees the faint infrared glow of distant galaxies, want the Sun, Earth and Moon all on one side of it?

## Analysis

1. Change the L2 distances from Part 1 into kilometers. For Sun–Earth the separation is 149.6 million km (1 AU); for Earth–Moon it is 384 400 km. How many times farther from Earth is the Sun–Earth L2 than the Moon is?
2. Change the L2 drift times into days. One period is 365.25 days for Sun–Earth and 27.32 days for Earth–Moon. Use them to answer Before the lab 2: about how often must a spacecraft near L2 correct its orbit?
3. Change the Trojans' L4 wobble period into years: one Jupiter period is 11.86 years. Could anyone watch a Trojan asteroid go once around its wobble in a lifetime?
4. From Part 2, between which two mass ratios does L4 stop being a place where objects stay? Turn that into words: how many times heavier must the big body be than the smaller one? Are the Sun–Jupiter and Earth–Moon pairs on the safe side?
5. From Part 2, what happens to the L1 and L2 distances as the smaller body gets heavier? Explain why in words.
