## Goal

See the three kinds of spectra, and use the lines in a spectrum to find out which elements are in a gas or a star.

## Background

**Spectra.** A spectroscope spreads light out by wavelength, from violet (about 400 nm) to red (about 700 nm), the way a prism or a rainbow does. Around 1860 Gustav Kirchhoff found that there are three kinds of spectra:

1. A hot, dense object (a glowing solid, liquid or dense gas, such as a lamp filament) gives a **continuous spectrum**: every color, with no gaps.
2. A hot, thin gas gives an **emission spectrum**: bright lines at a few wavelengths only, on a dark background.
3. A cool, thin gas in front of a hot, dense source gives an **absorption spectrum**: the continuous spectrum with dark lines where the gas has taken light out.

**Fingerprints.** The electrons in an atom can only have certain energies. When an electron drops from one energy level to a lower one, the atom gives off light of one exact wavelength; when it jumps up, it absorbs light of that same wavelength. Each element has its own set of energy levels, so each has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. A star is a hot, dense ball with cooler, thinner gas at its surface, so a star shows an absorption spectrum, and its dark lines tell us what the star is made of.

## Procedure

**Part 1. Kirchhoff's three spectra.** Choose hydrogen in Explore mode. Look at the strip with each of the three sources, Continuous, Emission and Absorption, and write down what you see for each (you will describe them in Analysis 1).

**Part 2. Bright lines and dark lines.** With hydrogen as an emission spectrum, record a reading of each of the four brightest visible lines (red, blue green, blue and violet). Then switch to absorption and record the same four lines, now dark. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 3. A fingerprint.** Choose sodium as an emission spectrum and record a reading of each of its two brightest yellow lines. Then choose neon and record a reading of its brightest line. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 4. A mystery star.** Choose Mystery mode and A star. Find four of the darkest lines in the star's spectrum and record a reading of each, then send the readings to the worksheet. Now find which elements made them: click elements in the periodic table to add them to your guess, which appears in the bottom strip, until every dark line in the star matches a line of your guess. Check your answer, and write down which elements you found.

## Analysis

1. Describe each of hydrogen's three spectra from Part 1 in one sentence, and say which source in the picture made each one: the hot lamp, the glowing gas, or the lamp shining through cool gas.
2. Compare the wavelengths of hydrogen's four bright lines with those of its four dark lines from Part 2. What do you notice? Explain it in terms of the electrons' energy levels.
3. In Part 3, what are the wavelengths of sodium's two yellow lines? How far apart are they in nanometers? Could you tell sodium from neon by these lines alone?
4. In Part 4, which elements did you find in the mystery star? For each line you recorded, name the element that made it.
5. One of the elements in the mystery star is hydrogen. Which of your recorded lines matches a hydrogen line from Part 2?

## Uncertainty

The wavelengths in the demo are measured values from NIST, given to a hundredth of a nanometer or better. A real hand spectroscope can be read to about 1 to 2 nm. Would that be good enough to tell sodium's two yellow lines apart? Would it be good enough to tell sodium's lines from hydrogen's red line? Explain.

## Questions

1. Sunlight shows thousands of dark lines. Which of Kirchhoff's three kinds of spectrum is it, and what does that tell you about the layers of the Sun's surface?
2. Helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, before it was found on Earth, and it is named after the Greek word for the Sun. How could anyone know an element was there before it had ever been collected?
3. A glowing cloud of gas in space shows a bright red line at 656.3 nm. What element is in the cloud, and is it hot thin gas or cool gas in front of a star?
4. Why does a light bulb's filament give a continuous spectrum, with no lines, even though it is made of atoms of one element, tungsten?
