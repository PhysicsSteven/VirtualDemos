## Goal

Compare the four giant planets with each other and with Earth, find out why Saturn's rings are rings and not a moon, and see how three of Jupiter's moons keep each other in step and heat Io from the inside.

## Background

**Kepler's third law for moons.** A moon obeys the same law as a planet going round the Sun: the cube of its distance, $$a^3$$, divided by the square of its period, $$P^2$$, is the same for every moon of one planet. With $$a$$ in the same unit of length for every planet (kilometers, say), that number is bigger for a more massive planet, in proportion to its mass, so moons are how we weigh planets. In this worksheet $$a$$ is measured in radii of each planet and $$P$$ in days, so the number is the planet's mass divided by the cube of its radius, and Analysis 2 turns it back into a mass.

**Density** is mass divided by volume. Water is 1.00 g/cm³, ice about 0.92 g/cm³ and rock about 3 g/cm³. A planet or moon's mean density tells us roughly what it is made of: a moon of about 3 g/cm³ is mostly rock, and one of about 1 to 2 g/cm³ is a mix of rock and ice. Jupiter and Saturn, the gas giants, are mostly hydrogen and helium, squeezed tightly near their centers. Uranus and Neptune, the ice giants, have atmospheres rich in hydrogen and helium too, but most of their mass is heavier material inside, thought to be water, ammonia and methane ices and rock in proportions not yet well known.

**Tides.** A planet pulls harder on the near side of a moon than on the far side, so it stretches the moon along the line between them. Close enough to the planet, the stretch beats the moon's own gravity, and a moon held together only by its gravity is pulled apart. The distance where this happens is the **Roche limit**. It is farther out for a fluffier moon, because a less dense moon holds itself together more weakly.

**Resonances.** When one moon goes round exactly twice for every orbit of another, the two line up at the same places over and over, and their small tugs add up instead of averaging out. Io, Europa and Ganymede are in a 1:2:4 resonance. The tugs keep Io's orbit slightly oval, so its distance from Jupiter and the stretch on it change every orbit. The flexing heats Io the way bending a paper clip back and forth warms it.

**Part 2: the four giants.** In the Planets view, record a reading for each of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, and for Earth. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 3: tearing a moon apart.** In the Rings view, choose Saturn and give the test moon the density of solid ice, 0.92 g/cm³. Put it at 3.0 Saturn radii, start the clock and let it make 3 orbits. Pause and record a reading. Start again and repeat with the moon at 2.0, 1.6, 1.5 and 1.4 Saturn radii. Then make the moon rock, 3.0 g/cm³, put it at 1.5 Saturn radii and record a reading after 3 orbits. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

## Analysis

1. Graph $$a^3$$ against $$P^2$$ for Jupiter's four moons. Is it a straight line? Find its slope. Do the same for Saturn's moons in the second copy of the table.
2. The slope is the planet's mass divided by the cube of its radius (in some units). Divide Jupiter's slope by Saturn's, then multiply by the cube of Jupiter's radius over Saturn's radius (use the equatorial radii from the Planets table). This gives Jupiter's mass over Saturn's. Compare it with the masses in the Planets table.
3. Look at the densities of Jupiter's four moons, from Io outward. What is the pattern? Which moons are mostly rock and which have a lot of ice?
4. From the Planets table: which planet is less dense than water? Which of the giants is densest? How does each giant's density compare with Earth's?
5. Flattening is how much wider a planet is at its equator than from pole to pole. Which giant is flattest? Use its rotation period and density to explain why.
6. From the Rings table: between which two distances did the ice moon come apart? Was that inside or outside the orange Roche limit? What happened to the rock moon at 1.5 Saturn radii, where the ice moon came apart, and why?
7. Saturn's main rings reach out to about 2.27 Saturn radii, and its nearest big moon, Mimas, orbits at 3.08 Saturn radii. Use Part 3 to explain why there is a ring close in and moons farther out.
8. From the Io table: when you doubled the eccentricity from 0.002 to 0.004, how many times did the heating go up? From 0.004 to 0.008? What rule does that suggest? What happens at $$e = 0$$?

## Uncertainty

The demo's numbers come from measured data and from calculations, so a repeated reading gives the same values. The uncertainty lies in what the models leave out. The test moon is a pile of smooth, frictionless boulders, and the Roche limit drawn is for a moon with no strength at all; a real moon of rock with some strength can survive closer in. The tidal heating uses one measured number for how Io's interior flexes and turns flexing into heat, known to about ±20%. Io's measured heat output is at least 2.5 W/m². Compare your Io reading at $$e = 0.004$$ with that and say whether the two agree, given the uncertainty.

## Questions

1. Saturn's mean density is less than water's, yet near its center the gas is squeezed to many times the density of water. How can both be true?
2. Uranus spins tipped over on its side. What would the seasons be like at its poles during its 84 year orbit around the Sun?
3. Europa has an ocean of liquid water under its ice, and Enceladus has geysers. Both are in resonances. Explain how a resonance can keep a small, cold moon warm inside.
4. A comet that passes very close to Jupiter can be pulled into pieces, as Shoemaker–Levy 9 was in 1992. Use the Roche limit to explain why a comet, a loose ball of ice and dust, is easy to tear apart.
