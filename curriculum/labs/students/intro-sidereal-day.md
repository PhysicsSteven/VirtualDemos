## Goal

Find out why a day measured by the Sun is longer than one turn of a planet, and why a sundial does not keep the same time as a clock.

## Background

**Two kinds of day.** A planet's **sidereal day** is the time it takes to turn once compared with the distant stars. Its **solar day** is the time from one noon to the next, when the Sun is highest in the sky. They are not the same, because while the planet turns it also moves along its orbit. After one full turn, the Sun is no longer in quite the same direction, so the planet has to turn a little farther to bring the Sun back overhead.

**Counting through a year.** In one orbit, a planet that spins the same way it orbits faces the stars one more time than it faces the Sun. If it turns N times against the stars in a year, it has N − 1 noons. So the solar day is the year divided by N − 1, and the sidereal day is the year divided by N. Earth turns about 366.26 times against the stars in a year, so it has about 365.26 solar days.

**Earth's two days.** Earth's sidereal day is 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. Earth moves about 1° along its orbit each day (360° in about 365 days), and turning 1° more takes about 4 minutes (24 hours ÷ 360). That makes the solar day 24 hours, which is what our clocks keep.

**Sundials and clocks.** A sundial reads noon when the Sun crosses the north and south line in the sky. A clock keeps an average day of exactly 24 hours. Because Earth's orbit is an ellipse (Earth moves faster when it is closer to the Sun) and Earth's axis is tilted, the Sun's daily step against the stars is not the same all year. Some solar days are a little longer than 24 hours and some a little shorter, and the small differences add up, so a sundial can be up to about a quarter of an hour ahead of or behind a clock.

## Uncertainty

The demo computes the days from the planets' measured rotation and orbit periods, so a repeated reading gives the same numbers. The top view is not to scale. Your solar day in Analysis 2 comes from dividing two readings. If the time readout were rounded to the nearest hour, how far off could Earth's solar day be after one solar day? After ten solar days? Why does counting many days give a better answer?
