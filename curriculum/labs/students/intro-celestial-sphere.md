## Goal

Use a model of the sky as a great sphere around Earth to explain why the stars circle the pole star, why the height of the pole star tells you your latitude, which stars never set, how the Moon moves among the stars in a week, and how the pole star changes over thousands of years.

## Background

**The celestial sphere.** The stars are so far away that they all look equally distant, as if fixed to the inside of a huge sphere around Earth. Earth turns once a day from west to east, so the whole sphere seems to turn the other way, from east to west, about an axis through Earth's poles. The points on the sky right above Earth's North and South Poles are the north and south celestial poles. Halfway between them, right above Earth's equator, is the celestial equator.

**Two ways to give a position.** Altitude (the angle above the horizon) and azimuth (the compass direction, 0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west) say where to look from where you stand, right now. Right ascension and declination are fixed on the sphere, like longitude and latitude on Earth: declination is the angle north (+) or south (−) of the celestial equator, and right ascension runs eastward around the sky from 0 h to 24 h.

**Your latitude and the pole.** Standing at the North Pole, the north celestial pole is straight overhead. At the equator it sits on the northern horizon. In between, the north celestial pole stands as many degrees above your northern horizon as your latitude. Polaris lies within 1° of the pole today, so it barely moves.

**Circumpolar stars.** Every star circles the pole once a day. A star close enough to the north celestial pole never dips below the horizon: it is circumpolar. A star close enough to the south celestial pole never rises at all. Seen from latitude φ north, a star of declination δ reaches its highest altitude when it crosses the meridian (the line from north through the point overhead to south): 90° minus the difference between φ and δ, taking the difference as positive. A star of declination 20° seen from 32.5° N, for example, gets as high as 90° − 12.5° = 77.5°.

**The Sun and the Moon.** Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° to its orbit, so the Sun's yearly path among the stars, the ecliptic, is tilted 23.4° to the celestial equator. The Moon moves eastward among the stars much faster, once around the sky in 27.3 days, always within about 5° of the ecliptic.

**Precession.** The pull of the Sun and the Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge makes Earth's axis swing slowly around a cone, like a spinning top, once in about 26,000 years. The celestial poles move slowly among the stars, so the pole star changes.

2. From Ruston, what is the smallest declination a star can have and never set? (Hint: for a star to stay up all day, its distance from the north celestial pole must be less than the pole's altitude.)
3. The Moon goes once around the sky (360°) in 27.3 days. About how many degrees does it move among the stars each day? Right ascension runs 24 h around the sky, so 1 h of right ascension is 15°. About how much does the Moon's right ascension grow each day?
4. Make a guess before you look: was Polaris always the pole star, for example when the pyramids at Giza were built about 2600 BC?

## Uncertainty

The demo computes positions from a star catalog and from formulas for the motions of Earth, the Sun and the Moon, accurate to well under a tenth of a degree today. The altitude of Polaris is not exactly your latitude, because Polaris is not exactly at the pole: your readings at different places were taken at different moments of the star's daily circle. From its declination in the first table, how far is Polaris from the pole today, and how far could its altitude be from your latitude because of that? What does the demo leave out that would make every star near the horizon look about half a degree higher in the real sky?
