The crater's size comes from a scaling law fitted to experiments and computer models of impacts (Collins, Melosh and Marcus 2005). Small craters are simple bowls; bigger ones collapse into a flat floor with a central peak, and the biggest have a ring of peaks inside. The shapes drawn are simplified; the sizes are the law's. The model uses one law for bowls and another for flat floored craters, and they do not meet: just past the change (about 19 km across on the Moon) a slightly bigger asteroid gives a crater about 9 % smaller. ## Goal

See how an asteroid's size sets the size and shape of its crater, use crater counts to put ages on the Moon's surfaces, and find out why the small Moon went quiet long before Earth.

## Background

**Craters.** An asteroid hits at many kilometers per second, so it carries an enormous energy for its size. It blasts out a crater far wider than itself, typically 10 to 30 times its own diameter. In weak gravity (the Moon) the same asteroid makes a bigger crater than in strong gravity (Earth).

**The crater clock.** Asteroids keep falling, so a surface collects craters as it ages: the older the surface, the more craters it has. Counting the craters on a patch of ground, and dividing by its area, gives the number of craters per square kilometer. The Apollo and Luna missions brought back rocks whose ages are known from radioactive dating, from places whose craters had been counted; together they calibrate the clock. The clock is not a straight line: billions of years ago impacts were far more frequent.

**Saturation.** When a surface is very old, new craters land on top of old ones and wipe them out. The count then stops growing as fast as the clock says, and the age read from it comes out too young.

**Cooling.** A planet stores heat in its volume but loses it through its surface. A small planet has much more surface for each cubic kilometer of rock, so it loses its heat faster. As the inside cools, the cold outer lid thickens, and lava finds it harder to reach the surface.

## Before the lab

1. An asteroid 1 km across makes a crater about 20 km wide. Make a guess: how wide a crater does an asteroid 10 km across make? Exactly 10 times wider, more than 10 times, or less?
2. Surface A has 10 craters wider than 1 km on a patch of 1000 km². Surface B has 50 on a patch of 1000 km². Which surface is older? How many craters per km² does each have?
3. A ball of radius R has surface area 4πR² and volume (4/3)πR³. Divide the area by the volume. The Moon's radius is about one quarter of Earth's: how many times more surface does the Moon have for each cubic kilometer?
4. An asteroid hits the Moon at a slant, 20° from the ground, instead of 45°. Make a guess: seen from above, will its crater be round, or stretched out along the asteroid's path? Will it be bigger, smaller or about the same size as at 45°? Give your reasoning.

## Analysis

1. For each Moon crater in Part 1, divide the crater diameter by the asteroid diameter. Does the ratio stay the same as the asteroid gets bigger? Compare with your guess in Before the lab 1.
2. At about what crater size did the shape change from a simple bowl to a crater with a flat floor and a central peak?
3. Compare the 1 km asteroid on the Moon and on Earth. Which crater is bigger, and by how many times (divide the bigger crater diameter by the smaller)? Explain why, using gravity.
4. For each surface in Part 2, check the Craters per km² column: divide your count N by the area. The setups count craters down to different sizes, so these numbers cannot be compared from one surface to the next. Put the four surfaces in order from youngest to oldest using the age from the clock.
5. The maria are dark lava plains that fill huge old basins. Using your ages, did the lava come before or after the highlands formed? About how long after?
6. Copernicus and Tycho are bright, rayed craters. What do your ages say about how long the rays on the Moon last?
7. Choose Highlands again and press Show the right count. Compare its Set age of this surface with the Age from the clock in your table. Which is larger? Explain the difference using saturation.
8. From Part 3, today: by how many times is the Moon's cold lid thicker than Earth's? How does this connect with the Moon's maria all being billions of years old?

## Uncertainty

A count of N craters is uncertain by about √N, because where the asteroids land is random. Take your Mare Tranquillitatis count, work out √N, and find the age the clock gives for N + √N and for N − √N craters: with your Mare Tranquillitatis marks still showing, mark √N more craters (any craters will do) and read Age from your count, then unmark until N − √N are marked and read it again. How many million years is the age uncertain by?

2. Chicxulub, the crater of the impact that ended the age of dinosaurs 66 million years ago, is about 180 km wide. Using Part 1, about how big was the asteroid?
3. In Part 1 (6), was the crater at 20° round or stretched, and how wide was it next to the one at 45°? Hold that up against your guess in Before the lab 4. Think about where the asteroid's energy goes in the first moment of the hit, and use it to explain the crater's shape and what the slant did to its size.
