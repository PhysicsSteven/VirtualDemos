## Goal

See why the Moon goes through phases, measure the length of the month from new moon to new moon, and find out why eclipses happen only in certain seasons of the year.

## Background

**Phases.** The Sun always lights half of the Moon, the half facing the Sun. As the Moon goes around Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. At new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, and its lit half faces away from us. At full moon the Moon is on the far side of Earth from the Sun, and we see the whole lit half. Halfway between are the first quarter and third quarter, when we see half of the disk lit. Earth's shadow has nothing to do with the phases.

**The month.** The Moon goes once around Earth against the stars in 27.32 days. Meanwhile Earth moves along its orbit around the Sun, so the Moon needs about two more days to line up with the Sun again. The time from one new moon to the next is the synodic month, the month of the phases.

**Rising and setting.** Earth turns once a day, so the sky turns 15° per hour. The Sun rises at about 6 am at an equinox. A Moon that is 90° east of the Sun rises 6 hours after the Sun.

**Eclipses.** A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes in front of the Sun, which can only happen at new moon. A lunar eclipse happens when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow, which can only happen at full moon. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's orbit, so at most new and full moons the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun or Earth's shadow. An eclipse can only happen when a new or full moon comes near one of the nodes, the two places where the Moon crosses the plane of Earth's orbit. In the demo, a solar eclipse is possible when the Moon is less than 1.48° above or below that plane at new moon, and a lunar eclipse when it is less than 1.53° from it at full moon.

## Before the lab

1. Sketch the Sun, Earth and the Moon at full moon. Which side of the Moon is lit, and which side faces Earth?
2. The Moon goes through all its phases in about 29.5 days. By about how many degrees does it move around the sky away from the Sun each day?
3. Make a guess before you measure: at what time of day does the full moon rise? At what time does the first quarter moon rise?
4. If the Moon's orbit were not tilted at all, how often would there be a solar eclipse?

## Analysis

1. For the month of phases, name the phase at each reading (new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent). At which elongations is the Moon half lit, and at which is it fully lit?
2. At what time does the full moon rise and set? The first quarter moon? Compare with your guesses in Before the lab 3.
3. For the month of phases, by how many hours does moonrise move later from one reading to the next? Divide by the days between readings to find how many minutes later the Moon rises each day.
4. For the eclipse series, find the time from one new moon to the next. This is the synodic month. How much longer is it than the 27.32 days the Moon takes to go around Earth against the stars?
5. Which of your new and full moons could have an eclipse? What were the Sun's distance from the node and the Moon's height β at those moments?
6. What is the largest size of β in your eclipse series? Compare it with the tilt of the Moon's orbit.
7. How many days pass from the first eclipse season in your data to the next one? About how many months is that?

## Uncertainty

The demo's readings are exact for its model, so a repeated reading gives the same numbers. The model leaves things out: the real Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so a real new moon can come several hours earlier or later than in the model, and real rise and set times change with your latitude and the time of year. Which of your results would you trust most for the real Moon, and which least? Explain.
