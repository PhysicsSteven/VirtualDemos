## Goal

Find out why the ocean rises and falls twice a day, why the Moon raises bigger tides than the Sun even though the Sun pulls on Earth far harder, and why some weeks have bigger tides than others.

## Background

**The Moon pulls the near side harder.** Gravity weakens with distance. The Moon pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it, less on Earth's center, and least on the far side. What stretches the ocean is not the pull itself but these differences: compared with Earth's center, the near side is pulled toward the Moon and the far side is left behind, away from it. This difference is called the **tidal pull**. The ocean bulges out on both sides, and Earth turns under the two bulges.

**The tidal pull falls off faster.** The Moon's pull goes as one over the distance squared: twice as far, one quarter of the pull. The tidal pull goes as one over the distance cubed: twice as far, one eighth.

**The Sun raises tides too.** The Sun is 27 million times heavier than the Moon but 389 times farther away, and the cube of 389 is 59 million. At new moon and full moon the Sun's and Moon's bulges line up and add: **spring tides**, the biggest of the month. At first and third quarter they are at right angles and partly cancel: **neap tides**.

## Before the lab

1. If the Moon were twice as far away, how many times weaker would its pull on Earth be? Its tidal pull?
2. The Sun pulls on Earth about 180 times harder than the Moon does. Guess: is the Sun's tide bigger or smaller than the Moon's?
3. Most coasts have two high tides a day, not one. Guess why the side of Earth facing away from the Moon also has a high tide.

## Safety

No hazards in the virtual lab. At a real beach, check the tide table before walking out on tidal flats: the water can come in faster than you can walk.

## Analysis

1. From Part 1, divide the pull at 40 Earth radii by the pull at 80 Earth radii. Do the same for the tidal pull on the near side, and on the far side. The distance doubled; how many times weaker did each get? Compare with Before the lab 1.
2. At today's distance, how many times bigger is the Moon's pull on Earth's center than its tidal pull on the near side? The tidal pull is tiny. Why can it still move whole oceans?
3. Compare the near side and far side tidal pulls in each reading. What does this tell you about the size of the two bulges, and so about the number of high tides each day?
4. From Part 2, divide the Sun's tide by the Moon's tide. Was your guess in Before the lab 2 right? Explain how the Sun can pull harder yet raise a smaller tide.
5. The day's range at one place is 1.5 times the height of the tide there. Check this for spring tides with (Moon's tide + Sun's tide) × 1.5, and for neap tides with (Moon's tide − Sun's tide) × 1.5. How many times bigger is the spring range than the neap range?
6. From Part 3, how long is it from one high tide to the next? The Moon returns over the same city every 24 hours 50 minutes. Why is that 50 minutes longer than a day, and how does it explain your interval?

## Uncertainty

The demo computes the pulls exactly, so a repeated reading gives the same numbers. The bigger uncertainty is in the model: name two things about the real Earth that the demo leaves out and that make real tides differ from place to place.

## Questions

1. Tidal friction drags Earth's bulges a little ahead of the Moon. Their pull speeds the Moon along, and the Moon drifts away from Earth by about 3.8 cm each year. Will the tides in the far future be bigger or smaller? Use your Part 1 readings.
2. The Moon always shows Earth the same face. Long ago, Earth raised tidal bulges in the solid Moon, and friction slowed the Moon's spin until it turned once per orbit. Why would Earth's tides on the Moon have been much stronger than the Moon's tides on Earth? (Earth is 81 times heavier than the Moon.)
3. Io, Jupiter's closest big moon, is squeezed by tides so strongly that it has hundreds of active volcanoes. Using what you found in Part 1, why does being close to a giant planet matter so much?
