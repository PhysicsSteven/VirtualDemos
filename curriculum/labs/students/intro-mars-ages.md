## Goal

Use crater counts to put ages on four regions of Mars, find when Mars was most active, and see why Mars stayed active longer than the Moon but not as long as Earth.

## Background

**The crater clock.** A surface collects craters as it ages, so the number of craters per square kilometer tells its age. On the Moon the clock is calibrated with rocks of known age brought back by Apollo and Luna. Nobody has yet brought back a rock from a known place on Mars, so the Martian clock is the lunar one adjusted for how often asteroids hit Mars compared with the Moon. Its ages are less certain than the Moon's, by a few hundred million years for old surfaces.

**Mars's three eras.** Geologists split Mars's history by its crater counts into the Noachian (heavily cratered, older than about 3.7 billion years, when water flowed), the Hesperian (about 3.7 to 3.0 billion years ago, with widespread lava and floods) and the Amazonian (the last 3 billion years, cold and dry, with fewer craters).

**Regions.** The southern highlands are rough and crowded with craters. The northern plains are smooth and low. Tharsis is a huge volcanic bulge carrying the largest volcanoes in the solar system, among them Olympus Mons, whose summit has a caldera (a collapsed crater left by lava draining away) about 80 km across.

**Crater size and gravity.** Mars's gravity is about 0.38 of Earth's and about 2.3 times the Moon's. Asteroids reach Mars more slowly than they reach the Moon, around 10 km/s against about 18 km/s.

## Procedure

**Part 1: dating Mars.** In Count craters, choose each Martian surface in turn: Southern highlands, Northern plains, Tharsis lava plains and Olympus Mons caldera. Keep the size to count that the setup chooses. Mark every crater at least as wide as the purple circle whose center lies inside the square, then record a reading. Send the four readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 2: the same asteroid on Mars and the Moon.** In Make a crater, drop a dense rock asteroid 1 km across coming in at 45° on Mars at 10 km/s, on Mars at 18 km/s, and on the Moon at 18 km/s, and record each crater once it has formed. Send the readings to the worksheet, then clear them.

**Part 3: Mars cooling.** In Cooling, set the time to 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 and 4.5 billion years after the planets formed and record a reading at each. Send them to the worksheet.

## Analysis

1. Put the four Martian surfaces in order from oldest to youngest by their ages. Compare with your ranking in Before the lab 1.
2. Name the era (Noachian, Hesperian or Amazonian) each surface belongs to.
3. The northern plains are about how much younger than the southern highlands? What does that say about how the north got its smooth surface?
4. The Olympus Mons caldera is the youngest surface you dated. What fraction of Mars's 4.5 billion year history ago was it last flooded with lava?
5. From Part 2, how much bigger is the crater on Mars at 18 km/s than on the Moon at 18 km/s? Explain using gravity. Then compare Mars at 10 km/s with the Moon at 18 km/s: which effect wins?
6. From Part 3, how did the cold lid of Mars change from 0.5 to 4.5 billion years? Compare Mars's lid today with the Moon's and Earth's.

## Uncertainty

A count of N craters is uncertain by about √N. For your count on the Olympus Mons caldera, work out √N and the age the clock gives for N + √N and N − √N craters (use the graph, or count a New surface). The Martian clock itself is uncertain by a few hundred million years for old surfaces. Which matters more for the Olympus Mons caldera, the counting or the clock? Which for the southern highlands?

## Questions

1. Mars is bigger than the Moon but smaller than Earth. Use your Part 3 results to explain why Mars had volcanoes long after the Moon's maria stopped erupting, while Earth still has them today.
2. Why are the ages from Martian crater counts less certain than lunar ones? What mission would fix that?
3. Wind, water and ice wear down small craters on Mars much faster than on the Moon. Would that make a crater count age too old or too young? Which size of crater would you trust most?
4. The demo's surfaces are simulated from the same clock you read the ages from. What part of this lab still tells you something real about Mars?
