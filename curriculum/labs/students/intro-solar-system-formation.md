## Goal

Follow the steps that turned a cloud of gas and dust into our solar system, and use them to explain why the planets near the Sun are small and rocky while the ones far out are giants.

## Background

**A spinning cloud.** Stars and planets form inside cold clouds of gas and dust. When a dense part of a cloud starts to fall in on itself, it is always turning a little. As it shrinks it spins faster, just as a figure skater spins faster on pulling in their arms: the spin (called angular momentum) cannot be lost, so a smaller spinning object must turn faster. Gas that falls straight along the spin axis can reach the young Sun, but gas that falls from the sides cannot get closer than a certain distance before it is moving sideways fast enough to stay in orbit. Gas falling from above and below meets in the middle, so the cloud flattens into a spinning disk.

**Hot near the Sun, cold far out.** The young Sun heats the disk, and the temperature falls with distance. Each material turns from gas to solid at its own temperature, called its condensation temperature. Metal and rock condense even at over 1300 K, but water freezes into ice only below about 180 K. The distance where the disk is cold enough for water ice is the **frost line** (or snow line). Beyond it, ice adds to the solids, and there is far more ice than rock in a gas of the Sun's makeup.

**Planetesimals and planets.** Dust grains stick together into bodies called **planetesimals**, from about a kilometer up to hundreds of kilometers across. Their gravity pulls them together, and they join into larger bodies. A body grows until it has swept up the solids in a ring around its orbit; the mass it ends with is called its isolation mass. A core of about 10 Earth masses is heavy enough to pull in the hydrogen and helium gas around it and become a giant planet, but only if the gas is still there. Young stars lose their disks' gas within a few million years.

**Melting from inside.** The first solids in the disk contained aluminum 26, a radioactive kind of aluminum with a half life of about 0.73 million years. Its decay heats a planetesimal from inside. If the planetesimal formed early enough, while plenty of aluminum 26 was left, its inside could melt, and the heavy iron would sink to the center to form an iron core. Iron meteorites are pieces of such cores.

## Uncertainty

Pick one stage and one thing its model leaves out (it is listed under The model and its formulas in the demo's Lab section).
