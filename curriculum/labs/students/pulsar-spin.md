## Goal

Follow a dying star's core as it collapses into a neutron star: find how fast it ends up spinning, how strong its magnetic field and how dense it becomes, then see why we see it as a pulsar, or don't, and how it slows down.

## Background

**A city sized nucleus.** When the iron core of a heavy star collapses, its electrons are crushed into its protons, and what is left is a ball of neutrons about 20 km across, holding more than the Sun's mass: a **neutron star**. Its average density is

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\tfrac43\pi R^3},$$

about the density of an atomic nucleus, $$2.7\times10^{17}$$ kg/m³. Neutrons resist being packed any tighter, the way electrons do in a white dwarf, and that holds the star up.

**Spin up.** Every star's core turns, slowly. As it shrinks it keeps its spin (its angular momentum), like a skater pulling in her arms: it turns faster. If its radius shrinks by a factor $$f = R_0/R$$, it turns $$f^2$$ times faster, so the time for one turn, the **spin period**, goes down by $$f^2$$:

$$P = \frac{P_0}{f^2}.$$

The same squeeze packs the star's magnetic field lines together, so the field grows by the same factor: $$B = B_0 f^2$$. A point on the equator moves at $$v = 2\pi R/P$$.

**The lighthouse.** A neutron star's magnetic poles are tilted from its spin axis, as the Earth's are. Charged particles stream out along the field and send radio beams from both magnetic poles. As the star spins, the beams sweep around the sky like a lighthouse's. Each time a beam sweeps across the Earth, a radio telescope sees a pulse. Such a star is a **pulsar**, and the time between its pulses is set by its spin.

**Slowing down.** A pulsar's spinning magnet loses energy to the radiation and particles it sends out, so it slows. The rate at which its period grows, $$\dot P$$ (seconds per second), is tiny but measurable. The energy it loses each second is what lights up the gas around young pulsars, such as the Crab Nebula. The Sun gives off $$L_\odot = 3.83\times10^{26}$$ W.

$$M_\odot = 1.99\times10^{30}\ \text{kg},\qquad 1\ \text{day} = 86{,}400\ \text{s},\qquad 1\ \text{yr} = 3.156\times10^{7}\ \text{s}.$$

## Uncertainty

The demo, like the pulsar catalog, assumes a neutron star of radius 10 km. Measured radii are closer to 12 km. Its moment of inertia, which sets the energy lost, goes as the radius squared for the same mass. By how many percent would the Crab's energy loss go up if its radius were 12 km instead of 10?
