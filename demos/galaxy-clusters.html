Galaxy Clusters and Dark Matter

Weigh a cluster of galaxies three ways: from how fast its galaxies move, from its hot X-ray gas, and from how it bends the light of galaxies behind it. All three find far more mass than the stars and gas we see. Then watch two clusters collide, as in the Bullet Cluster.

The model and its sources

Everything here is simulated. The cluster is an isothermal sphere with a core: the mass inside radius r is M(r) = 2σ²r³/(G(r² + r_c²)), with core radius r_c = 50 kpc, the mass that a gas or swarm with the same speed spread σ in every direction makes for itself (a β model with β = 2/3; Cavaliere and Fusco-Femiano 1976). The slider sets the mass inside 2,000 kpc, which sets σ. Stars are 2% of the mass (Gonzalez et al. 2013, ApJ 778, 14), hot gas 13% (Vikhlinin et al. 2006, ApJ 640, 691) and dark matter the other 85%, spread like the total. "What pulls" keeps the speeds but lets only part of the mass pull.

Zwicky's speeds: 1,000 galaxies with random positions (density ∝ 1/(r² + r_c²) out to 5,000 kpc) and random speeds (a bell curve of spread σ in each direction), the steady swarm of this sphere. Each galaxy moves in the cluster's pull (leapfrog steps of 1 Myr); galaxies do not pull on each other. We see only speeds along the line of sight, from redshifts. For this sphere the circular speed far from the core is v = √2 σ, so the mass inside R is M = v²R/G = 2σ²R/G, larger than the true mass by R²/(R² + r_c²) = 0.06% at 2,000 kpc. The spread is measured from the galaxies seen inside 2,000 kpc, so it scatters by a few percent from run to run, as a real survey's does.

Hot gas: gas in balance with the cluster's pull at temperature T, with kT = μ m_p σ² and μ = 0.6 (Sarazin 1988). The pressure balance gives M(r) = 2kT r³/(Gμm_p(r² + r_c²)). The dots are a sample of the gas moving with spread σ; a gas in balance has the same density as such a swarm, which is why the sample shows the right picture (collisions are left out). Real X-ray brightness goes as density squared, so a real core looks sharper. Its glow peaks at Wien's λ = b/T.

Lensing: the projected mass inside R is π σ² R²/(G √(R² + r_c²)), so a ray at angle θ from the center bends to β = θ − θ_E θ/√(θ² + θ_c²), with θ_E = 4π(σ/c)² D_ls/D_s and θ_c = r_c/D_l. The picture is ray traced pixel by pixel. A ring forms at θ = √(θ_E² − θ_c²) when the source is right behind the center, and the mass inside it is (c²/4G)(D_l D_s/D_ls)θ². Distances: a flat universe with H₀ = 67.4 km/s/Mpc and Ω_m = 0.315 (Planck Collaboration 2018), the lens at z = 0.296, the redshift of the Bullet Cluster.

The Bullet Cluster (a simple model, simulated): two Plummer spheres of dark matter, 1.0 × 10¹⁵ and 1.0 × 10¹⁴ solar masses (scale radii 300 and 150 kpc), fall together from 2,000 kpc, starting at 1,000 km/s; each moves rigidly in the other's pull (softened by both radii). Their galaxies are test particles. Each cluster's gas is one cloud with 13% of its mass that feels the same pull plus a drag while the clouds overlap, equal and opposite on the two (momentum is kept). The drag strength was chosen to make the picture look like the real one (Clowe et al. 2006, ApJ 648, L109; Markevitch et al. 2004). The real collision happened about 150 million years before the light we see left it.
