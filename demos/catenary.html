Hanging Chains and Arches

A chain hanging under its own weight takes the shape of a hyperbolic cosine, the catenary. Turn it upside down and you have the arch that stands in pure compression.

SI units · lengths in m, forces in N · g = 9.80665 m/s²

Setups

Chain

Taut: the length equals the distance between the supports. A perfectly straight heavy chain would need infinite tension, so the length is held at that distance.

Tension

Every bit of chain pulls on its neighbors along the chain. The horizontal part H = λga is the same everywhere, because nothing pushes sideways. The vertical part at any point holds up the chain between there and the lowest point: V = λg s. Together T = λg × (height above the directrix).

Free body diagram

Drag the two markers on the chain to choose a piece. Only three forces act on it: the pulls at its two ends and its weight. Placed tip to tail they close into a triangle, so the piece is in equilibrium.

Chain vs parabola

The parabola goes through both supports and has the same lowest height as the chain. A cable that carries a load spread evenly along the horizontal, like a bridge deck, hangs as a parabola. A chain carries its own weight spread evenly along its length, and that gives the catenary. For a shallow chain the two differ by about d³/(12L²), with d the sag and L the half span.

Bridge drawing: the main cable is the parabola, assuming the deck's weight per horizontal meter is much larger than the cable's own weight (the cable's weight is ignored). The faint curve is the bare chain. The force readouts above are for the bare chain.

Arch

Turn a hanging chain upside down and every pull becomes a push of the same size along the curve. An arch of uniform blocks in this shape carries its own weight in pure compression, with no bending: the line of thrust runs along the middle of every block.

Gateway Arch, St. Louis: 630 ft tall and 630 ft wide at the outside. Its legs are heavier than its top, so it is a weighted (flattened) catenary. The centroid curve on the original drawings is y = 693.8597 − 68.7672 cosh(0.0100333 x) ft for |x| ≤ 299.2239 ft (R. Osserman, Notices of the AMS 57, 220, 2010). The gold dashed curve is that formula scaled to this arch's span and rise.

Semicircle (qualitative): blocks in a half circle of the same span, with the dashed chain shape of the same span and rise. Hooke's rule: the arch stands if a hanging chain loaded like the arch fits inside the blocks. The dashed curve is a uniform chain, not the exact thrust line of these blocks, so read the red stretches as where the push wants to leave thin blocks. Heyman found that a semicircular arch needs a thickness of about 0.11 of its radius to stand.
