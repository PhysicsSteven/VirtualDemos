Quantum Coin Toss

A tossed coin already shows heads or tails before you look. A quantum coin, or the spin of an atom, in a superposition does not: measuring it gives each answer with probability |amplitude|², and leaves it in the state it was found in.

The model and its formulas

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|↑⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2)|↓⟩ P(up along n) = (1 + s·n)/2 = cos²(γ/2)

Model: a two state system (a qubit). The quantum coin's heads and tails are the two states |H⟩ and |T⟩, with a = cos(θ/2) and b = e^(iφ) sin(θ/2), so P(heads) = |a|² = cos²(θ/2) and the phase φ does not change it. A spin ½ state is the same mathematics: it points along the unit vector s = (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ) on the Bloch sphere, and a Stern–Gerlach magnet with its field gradient along n sends the atom up with probability (1 + s·n)/2, where γ is the angle between s and n (OpenStax University Physics Vol 3 §8.3, §7.4). After a measurement the state is the one found: along +n or −n. An atom from the oven is unpolarized, a mixture that gives up and down equally along any axis; it is drawn as a random direction, which has the same statistics. A beam that is let through goes into the next magnet; a blocked beam is stopped. Every magnet counts as a measurement: its two beams are never brought back together, so with Both the next magnet receives a mixture of the two results, not the state that went in. Every outcome is drawn at random (seeded generator, mulberry32), so the counts are simulated. The ordinary coin is a classical model: its face is decided by the toss, before anyone looks. Spin magnitudes, field strengths and beam sizes are not to scale, and the two beams leaving a magnet are drawn splitting up and down whatever its axis (a schematic).
