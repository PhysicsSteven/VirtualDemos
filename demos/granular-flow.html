Granular Flow: Sorting and Landslides

Mix sand of different sizes, then turn it, shake it, shear it or let it slide. The grains sort themselves: the small ones fall through the gaps between the big ones, and the big ones ride to the top and to the front.

The model and its formulas

The grains move in the plane of the picture, a layer one grain thick between two glass plates; rubbing on the front and back plates is left out. In the drum, shaken box, shear box and landslide each grain is a disk (a short cylinder) 3 mm thick, so a grain's weight goes as its area, as in a truly flat layer; with spheres in one flat layer a big bead would weigh more for the room it takes than a small one, and that would sort them by weight as well as size. In the sand picture each grain is a sphere, so that it settles through the liquid as a real sand grain does. Beads overlap slightly when they press together and push apart with a spring and a damper along the line of centres (a soft sphere model, Cundall and Strack 1979). The damper gives a coefficient of restitution of about 0.5 (set for two of the smallest beads; where a collision falls between time steps moves it by a few hundredths). The springs are much softer than real glass (kn = 3000 m g/d for the smallest bead) so the computer can take longer time steps; overlaps stay below a few percent of a diameter, and the flow does not depend on the stiffness at this level. Sideways, a spring of stiffness 2/7 kn resists sliding until the force reaches μ Fn, then the beads slide (Coulomb friction); μ of a pair is the mean of the two beads' roughness. Round beads resist rolling with a torque of up to 0.02 Reff Fn, angular grains with 0.2 Reff Fn (a pair uses the mean), a common way to model the corners of real sand grains in a model with round ones. Walls are rows of beads glued to the apparatus, so they are rough and have friction 0.5. Densities: glass 2500, quartz sand 2650, steel 7850, acrylic 1190 kg/m³. Each grain's size is varied at random by at least ±3 % (the size spread sets more), so equal grains do not lock into a perfect crystal. The time step is a tenth of the duration of a collision between two of the smallest beads; the simulation runs as fast as this device allows, up to real time, and the clock shows simulated time.

Rotating drum: the drum turns at ω; above ω²R/g = 1 the beads ride round with the wall (the critical rate). Shaken box: the box moves y = A sin(2πft); beads lift off the floor once Γ = A(2πf)²/g passes 1. Shear box: the lid moves at speed U and rests on the beads with its weight (its up and down motion is lightly damped); the box repeats sideways, so beads leaving one side come back on the other. Landslide: the gate lifts at 1 m/s at the start. Sand picture: the liquid moves with the frame as a solid body; each bead feels its weight less the buoyancy and a drag from the liquid (Schiller and Naumann, valid for Re below about 800), and a turning drag πηd³ω. The flow of the liquid around and between the beads is left out. Liquid data at 20 °C, glycerol in water by mass (Glycerine Producers' Association, Physical Properties of Glycerine and its Solutions, 1963): water 998.2 kg/m³, 1.002 mPa·s; 50 % 1126 kg/m³, 6.00 mPa·s; 75 % 1196 kg/m³, 35.5 mPa·s; 90 % 1235 kg/m³, 219 mPa·s; glycerol 1261 kg/m³, 1412 mPa·s. Simulated data; every run with the same settings is the same.
