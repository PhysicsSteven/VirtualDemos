The model and its formulas

altitude h: sin h = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H, H = LST − α

highest altitude 90° − |φ − δ|, lowest |φ + δ| − 90°; never sets when δ > 90° − φ (north)

Directions are worked out as vectors. The Earth turns by the Earth rotation angle of the IERS Conventions (2010), measured from the Celestial Intermediate Origin, which the demo carries along the moving pole; the pole and the ecliptic move by the long term precession of Vondrák, Capitaine and Wallace (2011), valid for 200 000 years either side of 2000. Nutation (up to 17″) is left out, and so are the aberration of starlight for the stars (up to 20″; the Sun's place includes it) and refraction, which lifts things at the horizon by about half a degree in the real sky: here the Sun sets when its center reaches the horizon. Star places are the catalog's J2000 positions moved along their proper motions (no radial velocity). The Sun is from Meeus ch. 25 (about 0.01°), the Moon from Meeus ch. 47 (about 10″), seen from your place on the Earth, which matters for eclipses. Eclipses compare the two disks as seen from your place (solar) or the Moon with the Earth's shadow, its radii from the Explanatory Supplement (1961) (lunar); the Moon is taken as a smooth sphere, so contact times agree with NASA's predictions to within about a minute: totality at Dallas on April 8, 2024 runs from 18:41:05 to 18:45:10 UT here and from 18:40:43 to 18:44:35 UT in NASA's tables. Both are fits made for dates within a few thousand years of 2000, so far from today they are approximate; precession and the stars do not depend on them. Times are standard time for the time zone nearest your longitude (longitude/15, rounded), with no daylight saving; ΔT = TT − UT from Espenak and Meeus (2006), within about 5 s of the measured value today and an estimate in the far past and future. Dates use the Gregorian calendar extended back in time, with year 0 for 1 BC. The pictures: the horizon view is a stereographic projection (shapes kept, sizes growing toward the edges), the whole sky is an azimuthal equidistant map with the zenith at the center, and the star chart plots right ascension against declination of date. Star dots grow with brightness; the Sun and Moon are drawn larger than life except in the eclipse inset, which is to scale.

Data

Stars: the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, 5th revised edition (Hoffleit and Warren 1991; CDS catalog V/50), every star to visual magnitude 5.5 (2887 stars): J2000 positions, magnitudes, colors and proper motions. Star names from the IAU Working Group on Star Names. Constellation boundaries: the IAU boundaries (Delporte 1930) as tabulated by Roman (1987; CDS catalog VI/42), used to name the constellation of any point. The figures joining the stars are the usual simple outlines, drawn for this demo.
