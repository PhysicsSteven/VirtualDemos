About the demo

Phase diagram view: each line is where two phases can sit together, drawn from the international reference equations for the measured boundaries. Water: the vapor pressure of the liquid from W. Wagner and A. Pruss (1993), and the sublimation and melting (ordinary ice, ice Ih) pressures from W. Wagner, T. Riethmann, R. Feistel and A. H. Harvey (2011), both adopted by IAPWS; triple point 273.16 K and 611.657 Pa, critical point 647.096 K and 22.064 MPa. Ice Ih melts down to 251.165 K at 208.566 MPa, where ice III takes over; the denser ices at higher pressures are left out, and the diagram stops at 200 MPa. Carbon dioxide: R. Span and W. Wagner (1996); triple point 216.592 K and 0.51795 MPa, critical point 304.1282 K and 7.3773 MPa. On these scales water boils at 99.97 °C at 1 atm (373.12 K); textbooks round it to 100 °C, as the Beaker view does. The pressure axis is logarithmic: each grid line is ten times the one below. Play heats (or cools) the sample at the dot's pressure: T moves at a steady rate between the lines and holds for 3 s at each line while the phase changes; the hold stands for the latent heat, and its length is not to scale. The molecule sketch beside the diagram is a picture of the arrangement, not a simulation; the Atoms view simulates argon atoms through the same changes. Pressure presets: the top of Everest (8,849 m) in the US Standard Atmosphere 1976, 31.4 kPa; a pressure cooker held 1 atm above sea level air, 202.65 kPa.

Vapor view: water in a sealed jar of air, or in an open pot. The saturation vapor pressure of water, p_sat(T), is from W. Wagner and A. Pruss (1993), adopted by IAPWS (2.339 kPa at 20 °C; 101.42 kPa at 100 °C). The vapor is an ideal gas, so its density is ρ = pM/(RT) with M = 18.015 g/mol. Molecules leave the water at a rate set by its temperature and come back at a rate in proportion to the vapor pressure, so p rises toward p_sat as 1 − e^(−t/2.5 s); the time constant is chosen so the jar saturates in seconds, where a real jar takes minutes to hours because the vapor has to diffuse through the air. Relative humidity is p/p_sat(T); the dew point is the temperature at which p_sat equals p. Cooled below it, the vapor above p_sat condenses at once on the glass as dew (in a real jar some fog can form first), so the relative humidity stays at 100 %. The thermostat moves the temperature of the water and the air together, at most 2 °C per second. In the open pot the water boils when its vapor pressure reaches the outside pressure, and the temperature then stays at that boiling point while the heat boils the water away. The sealed jar keeps the air it was closed with, so the pressure inside is the air's pressure (in proportion to the kelvin temperature) plus the vapor's, always above p_sat: it never boils. Outside pressures for Denver (1,609 m) and the top of Everest (8,849 m) are from the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 (83.4 and 31.4 kPa); a pressure cooker holds 1 atm above sea level air, 202.65 kPa. The arrows at the water's surface are in proportion to the rates at which molecules leave (p_sat) and return (p). Each drawn vapor molecule stands for 0.5 g/m³ of vapor, at most 260 drawn.
