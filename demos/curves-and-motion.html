Curves and Motion

Type a path as x(t) and y(t), as a polar curve r(θ), or as a curve in space, and watch a point move along it with its velocity and acceleration. The acceleration splits into a tangential part that changes the speed and a normal part that turns the motion, set by the curvature. Arc length and swept area build up as it goes, and the conics come from a focus and a directrix.

What you can type
  • Use t in x(t), y(t) and z(t), and θ (or theta) in r(θ).
  • + − * / ^ and parentheses; 2t, 3sin(t) and (t+1)(t−1) multiply.
  • sin cos tan, asin acos atan, sinh cosh tanh, exp or e^t, ln, sqrt, abs, pi
  • The range boxes take numbers or expressions such as 2pi or 40/9.81.
The model and its formulas

v = r′(t), a = r″(t), T = v/|v|, aT = a·T = d|v|/dt, aN = |v × a|/|v| = κ|v|², N = (a − aTT)/aN, κ = |v × a|/|v|³

s = ∫ |r′| dt, A = ½ ∫ (x y′ − y x′) dt = ½ ∫ r² dθ, conic: r = ep/(1 + e cos θ), directrix x = p

The boxes are read by a small parser written for this site (nothing you type is run as code). The first and second derivatives come from the exact rules of calculus applied to what you typed, so the velocity, acceleration and curvature at the point are exact. Arc length, area and the orbit's clock are integrals computed numerically by adaptive Simpson's rule, to about nine significant figures.

In a polar curve moving evenly in θ, θ plays the part of time, so the arrows are dr/dθ and d²r/dθ². Moving with equal areas in equal times, dθ/dt = h/r² with h constant, the motion of a planet under a force toward the origin.

Orbits: the Sun's GM is the IAU nominal value, lengths in astronomical units and time in Julian years. The planet's own mass and every other body are left out. The helix uses CODATA 2022 values for the proton, with the numbers in the boxes rounded to four significant figures.
