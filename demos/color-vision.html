Color Vision

Your eye has three kinds of cones, and every color you see is just how strongly light excites each of them. Mix red, green and blue lamps, compare a single wavelength, or put a filter in front of a white bulb.

The model and its formulas

L = ∫ S(λ) l̄(λ) dλ M = ∫ S(λ) m̄(λ) dλ S = ∫ S(λ) s̄(λ) dλ

Model: each cone signal is the light's spectrum S(λ) weighted by that cone's sensitivity and added up over 380 to 780 nm in 1 nm steps. The sensitivities are the CIE 1931 2° color matching functions (analytic fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013) turned into cone responses with the Hunt–Pointer–Estevez matrix normalized to daylight (Fairchild, Color Appearance Models), so daylight white at the brightness of the three full lamps gives L = M = S = 1. Two lights with the same three signals look the same, whatever their spectra (metamers). The lamps are modeled as Gaussian spectra 20 nm wide (full width at half maximum) at 630, 532 and 465 nm, and their powers are set so all three at 100% give exactly that white. One wavelength at 100% power has the brightness of that white at 555 nm, where the eye is most sensitive; the same power at another wavelength looks dimmer. Incandescent is a blackbody at 2856 K (CIE illuminant A, Planck's law); Daylight is CIE illuminant D65 (CIE 15:2004 table, every 10 nm, interpolated). The filter passes everything between its edges and nothing outside (an ideal band pass). The color in the bubble is the light's color in sRGB at its brightness; colors outside the screen's range are shown desaturated, so the purest spectral colors look paler than in life. Photons: each dot is one photon, its wavelength drawn at random from the spectrum in proportion to the number of photons (power times λ); the rate is slowed enormously to be seen. The eye adapts to the room in life; here nothing adapts.
