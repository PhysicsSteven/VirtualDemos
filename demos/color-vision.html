Color Vision

Your eye has three kinds of cones, and every color you see is just how strongly light excites each of them. Mix red, green and blue lamps, compare a single wavelength, put a filter in front of a white bulb, stack colored filters in any of the beams, mix paints, or light a stage with colored lamps.

The model and its formulas

L = ∫ S(λ) l̄(λ) dλ M = ∫ S(λ) m̄(λ) dλ S = ∫ S(λ) s̄(λ) dλ

Model: each cone signal is the light's spectrum S(λ) weighted by that cone's sensitivity and added up over 380 to 780 nm in 1 nm steps. The sensitivities are the CIE 1931 2° color matching functions (analytic fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013) turned into cone responses with the Hunt–Pointer–Estevez matrix normalized to daylight (Fairchild, Color Appearance Models), so daylight white at the brightness of the three full lamps gives L = M = S = 1. Two lights with the same three signals look the same, whatever their spectra (metamers). The lamps are modeled as Gaussian spectra 20 nm wide (full width at half maximum) at 630, 532 and 465 nm, and their powers are set so all three at 100% give exactly that white. One wavelength at 100% power has the brightness of that white at 555 nm, where the eye is most sensitive; the same power at another wavelength looks dimmer. Incandescent is a blackbody at 2856 K (CIE illuminant A, Planck's law); Daylight is CIE illuminant D65 (CIE 15:2004 table, every 10 nm, interpolated). The filter passes everything between its edges and nothing outside (an ideal band pass). The colored filters are ideal too: blue passes below 500 nm, green 500 to 600 nm, red above 600 nm; yellow, cyan and magenta each absorb one of those thirds (blue, red, green) and pass the other two. Real dyes have gradual edges and pass less than all of their band. Filters in a row multiply their transmissions, so the light after two filters is what both pass. The color in the bubble is the light's color in sRGB at its brightness; colors outside the screen's range are shown desaturated, so the purest spectral colors look paler than in life. Photons: each dot is one photon, its wavelength drawn at random from the spectrum in proportion to the number of photons (power times λ); the rate is slowed enormously to be seen. The eye adapts to the room in life; here nothing adapts.

Paints: model pigments, not measured ones. Each absorbs whole thirds of the spectrum (the same thirds as the filters), with smooth edges 26 nm wide (10% to 90%) whose absorption reaches 15 nm past 500 and 600 nm, as real paint absorptions overlap; a pigment reflects 88% where it does not absorb and 4% where it does. White reflects 88% everywhere, black 4% (up to 4.5% where two absorption edges meet). A thick coat of mixed paint follows Kubelka–Munk theory (Kubelka and Munk 1931; mixtures, Duncan 1940) in its single constant form, which takes every pigment to scatter alike: K/S = (1 − R)²/2R for each paint, the mix has K/S = Σ cᵢ (K/S)ᵢ by the parts cᵢ, and R = 1 + K/S − √((K/S)² + 2 K/S). Gloss at the surface is left out. The board is lit by daylight (D65) or the 2856 K bulb at the brightness of a white of Y = 1.

Stage lights: the three lamps are points on the floor at the front of the stage, 4 m from the backdrop, and they light the backdrop evenly (no fall off with distance or angle; all three at full give white of Y = 1 on a surface that reflects everything). The performer, 1.69 m tall, made of circles and rounded bars, stands d from the backdrop. By similar triangles a point at height z throws its shadow at height z m, m = D/(D − d), scaled about the lamp across the stage too. Point lamps give sharp shadows; a real lamp of some size gives a soft edge (penumbra). The front of the performer is lit by every lamp. The view is from the front, to scale, without perspective; the floor takes no shadows. Costumes and backdrops use the same model pigments as the paints, and each spot's light is lamp × reflectance.
