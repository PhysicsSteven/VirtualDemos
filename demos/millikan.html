Millikan Oil Drop

Spray tiny drops of oil between two charged plates and watch them through a telescope. Time a drop falling with the plates off, then rising or falling with them on: air drag tells you how big it is, and the field tells you its charge. Charge comes in whole numbers of one smallest amount, e.

The model and its formulas

plates off: 6πηr v_f / C = m′g, m′ = (4/3)πr³(ρ − ρ_air) plates on: qE = m′g + 6πηr v_E / C so q = 6πηr (v_f + v_E) / (C E)

C = 1 + (λ/r)[1.155 + 0.471 exp(−0.596 r/λ)] E = V/d held still: q = m′g d / V

Model: each drop is a sphere of oil, density ρ (886 kg/m³ for the light mineral oil used in teaching apparatus, as PASCO's Millikan manual gives; Millikan used clock oil), falling through still air between two flat plates d = 7.60 mm apart, a spacing typical of teaching apparatus (measure your own with a micrometer). The field between the plates is uniform, E = V/d, and points from the + plate to the − plate. A drop this small reaches its terminal speed in about 10 µs (its mass times C over 6πηr), so it always moves at the speed where the forces balance: weight less buoyancy, the electric force qE and air drag. Drag is Stokes's law 6πηrv divided by the slip correction C, because a drop not much bigger than the mean free path of the air molecules slips through the gaps between them; C is the fit of Allen and Raabe (1982) to Millikan's own oil drop data, with λ = 67.3 nm at 23 °C and 101.325 kPa, scaled to other temperatures and pressures as λ ∝ (T/p)(1 + S/T)⁻¹. Air: viscosity from Sutherland's law (η = 1.716 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s at 0 °C, S = 110.4 K, White, Viscous Fluid Flow), density from the ideal gas law (molar mass 28.96 g/mol), g = 9.81 m/s². Each drop also jiggles by Brownian motion, a random step of standard deviation √(2D Δt) in each direction with D = k_B T C/(6πηr) (Einstein 1905): that is why repeated times of one drop differ. The sprayed drops are simulated: radii drawn at random from 0.35 to 1.0 µm and charges of 1 to 12 e, mostly negative (spraying charges drops by friction). The ionizing source changes a drop's charge by one e at a time at random, about once every 8 s. The telescope shows a field of view 3.2 mm across round the middle of the gap, with lines every 0.5 mm; a real telescope turns the picture upside down, here it is shown upright. The apparatus picture on the left is not to scale: the drops are far too small to see without the telescope. Your own timing (the stopwatch) carries your reaction time; the orange lines time the drop exactly as it crosses them. Left out: the drops evaporating, the field's fringe near the hole and the plate edges, convection currents in the air, and the drops' charge images in the plates.
