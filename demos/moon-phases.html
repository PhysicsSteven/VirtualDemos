Lit fraction f = (1 − cos E)/2, where E is the Moon's angle from the Sun as seen from Earth.

Not Earth's shadow. A common idea is that phases come from Earth's shadow falling on the Moon. They do not. The Sun always lights half the Moon; we see different amounts of that lit half. Earth's shadow points away from the Sun and reaches the Moon only at full moon, during a lunar eclipse.

Rise and set times are approximate: an observer on the equator at an equinox, with the Moon on the celestial equator. Latitude and the Moon's tilted orbit shift real times by an hour or more, and the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day.

The Moon's orbit is tilted 5.1° to the ecliptic, so most new and full moons pass above or below the Sun line. An eclipse needs a new or full moon close to a node: the Sun within about 15 to 18° of a node for a solar eclipse, about 11° for an umbral lunar eclipse. The Sun passes a node every 173.3 days, giving two eclipse seasons a year. The nodes slide westward, once around in 18.6 years, so seasons come about 19 days earlier each year.

Model: circular orbits at steady rates, so phases are evenly spaced (the real Moon runs up to about 14 hours early or late). Sizes and distances are not to scale; the real Moon is about 30 Earth diameters away. In the view from Earth the terminator is drawn upright; in the real sky it tilts as the Moon crosses the sky. In the Southern Hemisphere the picture flips left to right.
