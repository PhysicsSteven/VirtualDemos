Chance Lab: Dice, Cards and Candies

Roll dice, deal cards with or without putting them back, and draw candies from a cup. Pick two events and watch every trial land in a Venn diagram, a tree diagram and a contingency table; check the rules of probability; and see the relative frequency settle toward the probability and the average toward the expected value as the trials pile up.

How it works

Every trial is simulated with a seeded random number generator (mulberry32, demos/lib/vdstats.js), so the same draw set always gives the same trials: Reset starts it again from its first trial, and New draw moves to the next set. Dice are rolled with replacement (each die is a fresh draw of 1 to 6). Cards come from one shuffled deck of 52; with replacement each card goes back and the deck is shuffled before the next draw. The cup holds 40 candies: 12 red, 8 green, 8 blue, 6 yellow, 4 brown and 2 orange, and two are drawn.

The probabilities are exact: the demo counts every equally likely ordered outcome (every way the first draws can come out) and writes P as a fraction of them. The distribution of X is exact too: a binomial distribution for draws with replacement, a hypergeometric one without (book 4.3 and 4.5), and a full count of the 6ⁿ outcomes for the sum of the dice. μ = Σ x·P(x) and σ = √(Σ (x − μ)²·P(x)); the sample mean x̄ and the sample standard deviation s (dividing by n − 1) come from your trials.

The shaded band in the Long run graph is μ ± 2σ/√n (for a relative frequency, p ± 2√(p(1 − p)/n)): by the central limit theorem about 95 % of running averages lie inside it once n is large (a normal approximation). Its narrowing as 1/√n is the law of large numbers at work.

Three dice: the book's lab (4.8) bets on a number and counts the matching dice, but leaves the payout to you. This demo pays as the old fairground game chuck-a-luck does: bet $1, win $1 for each die that matches your number, lose your $1 when none match.
