Charging by Induction

Bring a charged rod near two metal spheres without touching them. Free electrons in the metal move, so the near side and the far side get opposite charges. Separate the spheres, or ground one, before taking the rod away, and the charge stays.

More about this

Blind hides the net charges and potentials; the marks on the spheres stay, as an electroscope or a charge probe would show. In Conductor shapes, Blind hides the summary readouts, the E = 0 labels and the exact curve; the field lines, marks, graph and probe stay.

Model: two perfectly conducting spheres on ideal insulating stands, and the rod's charge as a point charge at its very tip, anywhere in the plane through the spheres' centers. Solved exactly with Kelvin image charges at every position as you move things (every image lies in that plane). Touching spheres are solved 0.5 mm apart and joined, so they are one conductor (B's share of a shared charge is within 2 % of true point contact for a 1 cm sphere B, and within 0.25 % from 3 cm up); a thin wire joins them the same way, its own charge neglected. A grounded sphere is held at zero potential, the potential far away. The bench, the stands, the wand, the leads and the rest of the rod are left out of the electrostatics: only their connections count. The color on each sphere is the exact σ on the side facing you, full color at ±1 µC/m² (its strength goes as the square root of |σ|); the + and − marks show σ around the rim in the plane of the drawing, darker for more charge per area. Real charge sits as a tiny excess or shortage of electrons, and only electrons move: each moving dot stands for 0.25 nC of electrons (1.6 × 10⁹), drawn slowed down (in metal the charge settles in far less than a microsecond), and the color eases to the new σ in about half a second for the same reason. The guided steps move the rod and B at a steady hand's pace. The largest field at each surface is σ/ε₀ where |σ| is largest around the rim; where it passes air's breakdown field, about 3 × 10⁶ V/m (OpenStax Vol. 2, Table 8.1), the picture marks where a spark would start but does not model the spark.

Model (Attracting a neutral insulator): a solid ball of radius 2.00 cm, uniform and linear with the dielectric constant of OpenStax Vol. 2, Table 8.1 (metal: a perfect conductor), and the rod's tip as a point charge on the line through the ball's center. The field and the force are the exact solution as a series of Legendre polynomials; the force is the sum of every multipole term, not only the induced dipole. The ball sits on a 2.00 cm insulating post on the pan; the balance, the post and the rest of the rod are left out of the electrostatics. The balance converts force to mass with g = 9.81 m/s² and reads to 0.01 mg. The molecules are drawn huge and their stretch is exaggerated; the + and − marks show the bound (or, in metal, free) surface charge density. At the smallest gap and 20 nC the field at a metal ball's surface stays below air's breakdown field, 3 × 10⁶ V/m.

Model (Conductor shapes): a 2D cross section. Each conductor is very long along the line of sight, so every field lies in the plane of the picture; the point charge becomes a line charge λ (charge per length, seen end on) and the conductor's net charge is per length too. The conductor is a perfect conductor, isolated, with the net charge per length you set (zero: neutral); its potential comes out of the solution. Solved by a boundary element method: the surface is cut into straight panels (the readout gives how many), each with a uniform σ; the potential is set equal at every panel's midpoint and the total charge is fixed, and one dense linear solve gives every σ. The field anywhere is then the exact field of those panel charges plus E₀ and λ, so E = 0 inside the metal and the cavity is not imposed: it comes out of the solution, and the readouts report what is left (the numerical error). Convergence, checked in the test file: doubling the panels changes the largest σ by less than 0.1 %, and the circle matches the exact σ = 2ε₀E₀ cos θ to better than 0.01 % of 2ε₀E₀. The teardrop's point is rounded to 1.5 mm and the box's corners to 3 mm (outside) and 1 mm (cavity): at a perfectly sharp point σ grows without limit. Field lines are traced from positive charges and from where the field enters the picture, one line per equal amount of flux. The probe compares σ on the nearest panel with ε₀E⊥ just outside it; with uniform σ on each panel they agree to within about 1 % of the largest σ.
