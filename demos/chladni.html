Model

Thin plate (Kirchhoff–Love) theory: D∇⁴w = −ρh ∂²w/∂t² with flexural rigidity D = Eh³/(12(1 − ν²)). Small, linear motion of a flat, uniform, isotropic plate. Good while the bending wavelength is long compared with the thickness (k h well below 1; shown under Plate).

Every edge is free. Two drives: Wave driver (the default for the center and off center posts) is a voice coil bolted to the plate: its coil pushes with force F_c on its own moving mass m_d = 50 g (an estimate) and on the plate, so the post moves x = F_c/(1/G − m_d ω²), with G the plate's point receptance at the post (the modal sum, rigid modes included, plus the share of the modes above the computed range from the plate's average mode density). The bolt is treated as a point; the clamp's width and the driver's suspension are left out. At low frequency the whole plate rides up and down with the post, |a| ≈ F_c/(M + m_d) everywhere. The plate with the driver's mass at its center has its own resonances, between those of a free plate and those of a plate pinned at the post. Point force (the default at the edge, like a bow) is an ideal force F₀ cos(2πft) on a free plate.

Circle: exact solution, w = [J_n(λr/a) + C I_n(λr/a)] cos nθ, with the free edge frequency equation solved to 12 digits. Square: no closed form; Rayleigh–Ritz with Legendre polynomials up to degree 30 in x and in y (961 terms, split into symmetry classes), converged to better than 1 part in a million for every mode shown.

Damping: every flexural mode has the same Q (default 50, a model choice: a plate bolted to a driver and carrying sand is heavily damped). The response is the sum over all computed modes, between resonances too. The sand's mass does not load the plate.

Drive level: PASCO publishes no force rating for its wave drivers. The SF-9324 manual gives amplitudes up to 7 mm peak to peak at low frequency, and its product page about 5 mm up to about 50 Hz; the WA-9855 takes at most 1.2 A. The demo takes full drive to be the coil force that moves the driver's 50 g alone 3.5 mm at 50 Hz, F = m_d (2π·50 Hz)² × 3.5 mm = 17 N, and never lets the post move more than 3.5 mm (the stroke limit). The Driver setting x₀ is the amplitude the setting would give the driver alone at 50 Hz. These numbers are estimates, not measurements.

Sand is phenomenological, not a granular simulation. Where the local acceleration amplitude |a| exceeds g the plate throws a grain off once a cycle, at the moment its acceleration passes −g, with launch speed v_L = (g/2πf)√((|a|/g)² − 1). It flies for t_f = 2v_L/g. A grain moves on average at v_L: when t_f is shorter than 1/60 s it moves v_L × 1/60 s every 1/60 s; when it is longer it lands only once per flight, a hop of v_L × t_f in a random direction plus a drift of up to 0.5 of a hop toward smaller |a|: full where |a| changes by a factor of 4 or more across a half plate width relative to itself (near nodes), proportionally less where the plate moves nearly as a whole. The drift stands in for grains bouncing off the tilted, moving surface, which carries them toward the nodes. Where |a| < g a grain stays put. So at low frequency, where v_L is large, sand hops far and the plate looks chaotic; at high frequency it buzzes and creeps. Grains in flight are drawn with a slight blur; grains that pass the edge fall off. The plate starts with 24,000 grains (12,000 on a phone, a tablet or a computer with 4 cores or fewer), each drawn about a pixel across: the count and the drawn size are a picture, not real sand. Very light powder (lycopodium) gathers at the antinodes instead, carried by air currents over the plate (Faraday, 1831); air is not modeled.
