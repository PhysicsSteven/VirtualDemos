Numerical Methods for Motion

A computer cannot solve F = ma exactly: it takes small steps in time. Run four common step rules side by side on a spring, a pendulum and an orbit, and see which errors come from the method and which from the physics.

The four step rules

Euler: x ← x + h v, v ← v + h a(x old)

Symplectic Euler: v ← v + h a(x), x ← x + h v new

Verlet: v ← v + ½h a(x), x ← x + h v, v ← v + ½h a(x new)

RK4: four slopes per step, k₁ at the start, k₂ and k₃ at the middle, k₄ at the end, weighted 1 : 2 : 2 : 1

Each rule turns the equation of motion a = F/m into a recipe for the next position and velocity from the present ones, h seconds later. Euler uses only the start of the step, so a curving motion keeps stepping off the outside of its curve: the energy grows every step. Symplectic Euler (also called Euler–Cromer) differs only in order: it updates v first and moves x with the new v. That small change makes it keep the area of every patch of the phase portrait, so its energy wobbles but never drifts. Verlet (velocity Verlet, the leapfrog method) splits the velocity kick in two halves around the move; it is symmetric in time, keeps phase space areas too, and is second order. RK4, the classic fourth order Runge–Kutta method, samples the slope four times per step and is far more accurate per step, but it does not keep phase space areas, so over many periods its energy drifts slowly (down, for these systems).

Order: halve h and a method of order p cuts its error after a fixed time by about 2ᵖ. Euler and symplectic Euler are first order, Verlet second, RK4 fourth. On a log–log plot of error against h, the slope is p, until rounding (about 10⁻¹⁶ per step in double precision) sets a floor.

The model and its formulas

Spring: a = −(k/m)x, m = 1.00 kg, k = 4π² m/T² = 39.5 N/m, T = 1.000 s, A = 0.100 m

Pendulum: α = −(g/L) sin θ, L = 1.00 m, g = 9.81 m/s², T = 4√(L/g) K(sin(θ₀/2))

Orbit: a = −GM r/r³, GM = 4π² AU³/yr², semimajor axis 1.00 AU, T = 1.000 yr

Error: spring √(Δx² + (Δv/ω)²), pendulum √(Δθ² + (Δθ′/ω₀)²), orbit |Δr| Energy error: (E − E₀)/|E₀|

Model: an ideal massless spring, a point bob on a massless rigid rod, and a planet of negligible mass around a fixed star; no friction or drag. Every run starts at a turning point: the block at x = A at rest, the bob at θ₀ at rest, the planet at perihelion. The step h is the period divided by N, so after N steps every method has gone exactly one period. The dashed exact motion is not stepped: the spring's is x = A cos ωt; the pendulum's is sin(θ/2) = sin(θ₀/2) cd(ω₀t) (Jacobi's elliptic function, from the arithmetic–geometric mean, to rounding); the orbit's comes from Kepler's equation M = E − e sin E, solved by Newton's method to rounding. The error is the distance between the method's point and the exact point at the same time: on the phase portrait for the spring and the pendulum (velocity scaled by ω so both axes are lengths or angles), and in space for the planet. A force evaluation is one use of a(x); Verlet reuses the end of one step as the start of the next, so it costs one per step, like the two Euler rules, while RK4 costs four. A run is stopped when its energy is off by a factor of 1,000 or the planet comes within 0.01 AU of the star; that is the method failing, not the physics. Arithmetic is ordinary double precision.
