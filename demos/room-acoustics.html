Room Acoustics and the Anechoic Chamber

Clap in a hard room and the sound keeps arriving from the walls long after the clap: the reverberation time T falls as you add absorption, as Sabine's formula T = 0.161 V/A predicts. Line every surface with wedges and the echoes vanish: in an anechoic chamber the level falls as 1/r², down to the wedges' cutoff frequency.

c = 343 m/s, air at 20 °C

More about this

Blind hides A, Sabine's and Eyring's T, the free field line and the wedge cutoff; the measured T and the level meter stay. Mystery panels always hide their α.

Model

Geometrical acoustics: N rays leave the loudspeaker in random directions, each carrying 1/N of the clap's energy (N = 4000 to 64000, more when T is short, so that every measurement collects about as many rays). At a surface a ray keeps (1 − α) of its energy and leaves in a random direction (Lambert's cosine law, a fully diffuse reflection). The microphone is a sphere 0.5 m in radius that adds up the energy of the rays crossing it; the direct sound is added exactly, 1/(4πr²). Energies add, so there is no interference: no room modes or standing waves, which matter in real rooms at low frequency. Air absorption is left out (it shortens T in large rooms above about 2 kHz).

The clap shows 1500 of these rays, by the same rules, each drawn fainter as its energy falls and gone at −40 dB. Slow motion runs the sound 100 times slower than life; Real time moves it at 343 m/s, so it crosses a 9 m room in 26 ms and dies away in about two thirds of T (the rays reach −40 dB at 40/60 of the time to fall 60 dB).

T is measured as in ISO 3382: the decay curve is the backward integral of the clap response (Schroeder's method), and a least squares line from −5 to −25 dB is extended to −60 dB (T20). A measured T scatters by about 1 to 3 % from run to run of the rays (each setup always gives the same run); the level meter by under 0.2 dB.

Sabine: T = 24 ln 10 · V/(cA) = 0.161 V/A, A = Σ Sα. Eyring: T = 0.161 V/(−S ln(1 − ᾱ)), which goes to 0 as ᾱ → 1. Panels are 1.2 m × 0.6 m, spread evenly around the walls; the ceiling is the wall material.

Wedges line all six surfaces (the floor is a wire net), and the room is the free space between their tips. Beranek and Sleeper (J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 18, 140, 1946) define the cutoff as the frequency where the pressure reflection rises to 10 % (α = 0.99) and find it where the wedge length is about a quarter wavelength: f_c = c/(4d). Around f_c this demo uses the stated model R = 0.1 (f_c/f)², at most 0.9, α = 1 − R²; the shape is a model, not measured data. The level meter reads the steady sound of a 10 mW source (sound power level 100 dB), with ρ = 1.204 kg/m³.
