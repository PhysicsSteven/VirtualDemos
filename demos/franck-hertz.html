More about this

Energy is exact: an electron at distance x past the control grid G1 has its starting energy plus e(U − Uc)x/L, minus every amount it has given to atoms. The path is drawn straight; the electron moves in a uniform field over the gap L (8 mm for the mercury tube, 5 mm for neon, typical values).

Once above threshold, an electron excites an atom after a random distance with mean λ = 1/nσx. σx is effective: elastic collisions make the real path zigzag, so an electron meets far more atoms per millimeter of progress than a straight path would. Elastic collisions are otherwise left out of the energy (each costs only 2m/M of E, 5 × 10⁻⁶ for mercury). They do two things kept here: the electrons reach G2 moving in random directions, so one with energy E gets past Ur with chance 1 − eUr/E, and a fraction is scattered out of the beam, e^(−nσsL), σs = 5.3 × 10⁻²² m² (effective).

Electrons start with thermal energy from a 1100 K oxide cathode plus a 0.5 eV spread standing for potential differences across the cathode and grid meshes. The current entering the gap rises with U as u3/2/(1 + u3/2), u = (U − Uc)/Us, Us = 8 V for mercury and 20 V for neon: space charge limits it at low U (Child's law), the control grid G1 at high U. It sets the rising baseline only, but it pulls each maximum slightly toward higher U. The nA scale resembles a typical tube; amplifier noise is 0.02 nA rms. Ionization is left out.

Vapor pressure of mercury from Huber, Laesecke and Friend, Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 45, 7351 (2006), with the vapor in equilibrium with liquid mercury at the oven temperature.
