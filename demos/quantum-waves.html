More about this

Tap levels in the diagram (or press 1 to 9 with the picture in focus) to add states or drop them; the chosen states get equal weights. One state alone is stationary: |ψ|² never changes and only the phase turns. States with different energies beat, once every h/ΔE for two. States with the same energy mix into another stationary state.

Without the field each cₙ just turns, cₙ(t) = cₙ(0) e^(−iEₙt/ħ), computed exactly. ⟨E⟩ = Σ|cₙ|²Eₙ is the energy in the well alone: with the field on, energy goes into and out of the light.

More about this

The light's electric field ℰ cos ωt pushes on the electron (charge −e) with energy eℰx cos ωt (or eℰy). Light only links two states when ⟨f|x|i⟩ is not zero: in the box x must change the parity of nx and leave ny alone, in the oscillator nx goes up or down by exactly 1, in the round box m changes by 1.

iħ dc/dt = (E + eℰX cos ωt) c is solved in the basis of the 36 lowest states (fewer where a group of equal energies would be cut) by fourth order Runge–Kutta, with a step under ħ/20E of the top state. The Rabi period h/(eℰ|⟨f|x|i⟩|) is the two level answer on resonance in the rotating wave approximation, close when eℰ|⟨f|x|i⟩| is far below the gap. 0.03 V/nm is 3 × 10⁷ V/m, the peak field of light at 1.2 × 10⁸ W/cm² (I = cε₀ℰ²/2).

Left out: spontaneous emission (here only the light can make the electron fall, as stimulated emission; real excited states decay on their own in nanoseconds, far longer than these femtoseconds), the light's magnetic field, and the change of the field across the well (its wavelength is hundreds of nanometers, the well a couple: the dipole approximation).

More about this

Δx and Δp are standard deviations. Once a packet splits into reflected and transmitted parts, Δx spans both pieces, so the product grows far above ħ/2.

A Gaussian packet ψ ∝ exp(−(x − x₀)²/4σ² + ik₀x). At launch Δx = σ and Δp = ħ/2σ, the smallest product allowed.

More about this

A plane wave has one energy. The packet carries a spread of energies, and T depends on energy, so the packet's T is the average of T(E) over its energy distribution. A broad packet (large σ) has a narrow spread and comes closest to T(E₀).

More about this

Measure x picks a position at random with probability |ψ|² and collapses ψ to a narrow packet there, standing in for a detector of finite resolution; then it spreads again. Measure 200 copies measures 200 identically prepared electrons at this same moment without touching this one; the histogram builds up |ψ|².
