Confidence Intervals

Draw a sample, build its interval, and stack it under the last one against the true value. About 95 of every 100 intervals at 95 % catch the truth; the rest miss, and you can see which. Try a small n with t and with z, a proportion, and watch the width shrink as 1/√n.

How it works

Every sample is simulated by a seeded random number generator; nothing here was measured. The pendulum: a 1.000 m simple pendulum at small amplitude, true period T = 2π√(L/g) with g = 9.81 m/s², timed by hand once a swing; each timing is T plus a normal error with σ = 0.080 s. Heights: normal with μ = 165.0 cm and σ = 7.0 cm. Times between decays: exponential with mean 2.00 s (and σ = 2.00 s), strongly skewed. Photons: each photon passes a polarizer at angle θ to the light's polarization with probability p = cos²θ (Malus's law), independently.

Intervals, as in the book: z with σ known, x̄ ± z(σ/√n); z with s puts the sample's s in place of σ, which the book warns against; t with s, x̄ ± t(s/√n) with n − 1 degrees of freedom; a proportion, p′ ± z√(p′q′/n) (a normal approximation to the binomial, good when np′ and nq′ are both above 5); plus four, the same with two successes and two failures added. The critical values z and t come from the exact normal and t distributions (demos/lib/vdstats.js).

The samples are kept, so changing the confidence level or the interval recounts the same samples. A new n, population, angle or draw set starts new samples. Each n has its own stream of random numbers, so the 100 samples at n = 10 are the same whatever you drew before.

Width against n: each dot is the mean width of the intervals drawn at that n, at the level and interval shown; the curve is 2z σ/√n (or 2z√(pq/n)) at that level, the width an interval with σ known would have.
