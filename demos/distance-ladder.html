The method and its data

m − M = 5 log₁₀(d / 10 pc), so d = 10^((m − M + 5)/5) pc

A main sequence star's spectral type tells its temperature, and on the main sequence that sets its true brightness M. The calibration is real: the Hipparcos stars within 25 pc whose spectra are classed main sequence (class V) in half class bins, from the HYG database v3.8 (CC BY-SA 4.0), whose Hipparcos distances are from van Leeuwen (2007). The ten target stars are real too, drawn at random by a fixed rule (tools/hyg-nearby/build.py): two per bin from the class V stars 40 to 100 pc away. Their parallax distances are the check.

Extinction by dust is ignored; within 100 pc it is small. A star whose type was misjudged, an unresolved pair, or a slightly evolved star all fool the method, and stars bright enough to be catalogued far away tend to be the more luminous ones of their type.

The method and its data

ridge line: MV = c₀ + c₁(B − V) + c₂(B − V)²
offset of a star = V − (m − M) − MV(B − V)

The clusters are simulated: their stars sit on that ridge line at a hidden distance, with 0.08 mag of spread in V and 0.02 in B − V, and 30% of them are unresolved pairs, brighter by up to 0.75 mag. No dust, so no reddening or extinction, and nothing fainter than V = 19. Real clusters need both corrections, and their stars' composition and age move the main sequence a little.

Inverse square law

The model and its formulas

MV = −2.43 (log₁₀P − 1) + a
m − M = 5 log₁₀(d / 10 pc)

Slope and zero point a = −4.05 from Benedict et al. (2007), who measured Hubble Space Telescope parallaxes of ten Milky Way Cepheids: rung 1 calibrating rung 2. Moving a moves every Cepheid distance, and everything above it.

The same light spreads over an area that grows as d², so the flux is F = L/4πd². Twice as far means a quarter of the flux, which is 5 log₁₀2 = 1.51 magnitudes fainter.

The method and its data

V = VC − 2.5 log₁₀(NV/NC)
σV ≈ 1.0857 √((σV/NV)² + (σC/NC)²), σ² = N + npix(sky + 5²)

Simulated CCD frames, one per night, of the same Cepheids as rung 2, so the nights are rung 2's nights. The field is uncrowded: no other star lies within 14 px of the Cepheid or a comparison star. Each star is a Gaussian blur of the night's seeing (2.2 to 4.0 px wide at half maximum) on a flat sky, dimmed by the night's thin cloud (transparency 60 to 100%), with photon noise and 5 e⁻ of read noise (gain 1 e⁻ per count). A star of V = 25 gives 1 count on a clear night. The comparison stars' V magnitudes (C1 13.85, C2 14.62, C3 15.10) are given, as if from a standard star calibration.

An aperture sums the pixels whose centers lie inside it and takes away the sky, the median of a ring 3 to 8 px outside it. Dropping an aperture centers it on the star's light, as photometry software does. Clouds and seeing change both stars' counts by the same factor, so the difference cancels them; one aperture size for both stars also cuts off the same share of each star's light.

More about this

The supernova peak M is measured in galaxies with Cepheid distances, so shifting a shifts it by the same amount. A calibration 0.1 mag too bright makes every distance 4.7% too large and H₀ 4.5% too small.

Measured values today: about 67 to 68 km/s/Mpc from the cosmic microwave background with the standard cosmological model (Planck 2018: 67.4 ± 0.5), and about 73 from the Cepheid and supernova ladder (SH0ES, Riess et al. 2022: 73.0 ± 1.0). Other ladder calibrations, such as the tip of the red giant branch, give values near 70. Why they differ is not settled.

Reading the ladder

More about this

Bars show roughly where each method works well; the faded ends are where it still works with larger errors. Where two bars overlap, the same objects can be measured both ways, and that overlap is how the farther method is calibrated.

An error in a low rung carries up to every rung above it. That is why so much effort goes into parallaxes of nearby Cepheids.
