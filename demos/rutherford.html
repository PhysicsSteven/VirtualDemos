Rutherford Scattering

Fire alpha particles at a gold foil. A few bounce straight back, which Thomson's plum pudding atom cannot do: the positive charge must sit in a tiny nucleus.

Model

Pure Coulomb force, nonrelativistic: repulsion for the alpha, attraction for the antialpha. The trajectory is the exact hyperbola (for attraction, the branch that wraps around the nucleus), and the projectile moves along it on the exact timing (Kepler's equation for a hyperbola). The nucleus is held fixed. The recoil readout shows the lab angle and closest approach when it is free to move: at the same b the angle changes by under 1% even for aluminum, and the closest approach grows by about 2% for gold and up to 15% for aluminum. The strong force is not simulated, only flagged; neither is annihilation, which ends any antialpha that reaches the nucleus. The antialpha is the antimatter partner of the alpha, with the same mass; only the sign of its charge is changed here.

Why not a negative ion? An O⁻ or H⁻ ion holds its extra electron by only about 1.5 or 0.75 eV, so it loses that electron in the first atomic layers of the foil, and at MeV energies it is stripped further and reaches the nuclei positive. At the nucleus only the net charge counts, so a real negative projectile has to be a bare negative particle, like an antiproton or a negative muon.

Beam mode is a 3D beam: impact parameters are spread evenly over a disk, so their density grows as b, and each track is drawn in its own plane of scattering. The detector ring stands for Geiger and Marsden's zinc sulfide screen and microscope, which really sat centimeters away. Each alpha is scattered by one nucleus; the many distant, small deflections in a real foil are left out.
