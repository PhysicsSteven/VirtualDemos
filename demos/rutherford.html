An alpha is a helium nucleus, z = 2, mc² = 3727.4 MeV. Geiger and Marsden's 1913 source was radon with its short lived decay products, giving alphas of 5.49, 6.00 and 7.69 MeV; the fastest, from ²¹⁴Po (radium C′), is the default here. Many textbooks quote 5.5 MeV.

Model

Pure Coulomb repulsion, nonrelativistic. The trajectory is the exact hyperbola, and the alpha moves along it on the exact timing (Kepler's equation for a hyperbola). The nucleus is held fixed. The recoil readout shows the lab angle and closest approach when it is free to move: at the same b the angle changes by under 1% even for aluminum, and the closest approach grows by about 2% for gold and up to 15% for aluminum. The strong force is not simulated, only flagged.

Beam mode is a 3D beam: impact parameters are spread evenly over a disk, so their density grows as b, and each track is drawn in its own plane of scattering. The detector ring stands for Geiger and Marsden's zinc sulfide screen and microscope, which really sat centimeters away. Each alpha is scattered by one nucleus; the many distant, small deflections in a real foil are left out.
