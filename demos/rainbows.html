Rainbows

Follow one ray of sunlight into a raindrop, around the back and out again, and see why the light leaving the drop piles up near 42° from the point straight opposite the Sun. Then step outside: the primary bow, the fainter secondary bow with its colors reversed, the dark band between them, and the cone of drops that makes your own rainbow.

The model and its formulas

sin i = n sin r D = 2(i − r) + k(180° − 2r) least D where cos² i = (n² − 1)/(k² + 2k)

Model: a raindrop is a sphere of pure water at 20 °C, its index n(λ) from the Sellmeier formula of Daimon and Masumura (2007), as in the Optics Bench (n = 1.3334 at 589 nm, 1.3436 at 400 nm, 1.3305 at 700 nm). Sunlight arrives as parallel rays (geometric optics). A ray at impact parameter b (its distance from the drop's center line, as a fraction of the radius) meets the surface at i with sin i = b, refracts by Snell's law, reflects k times inside and leaves; it has turned through D = 2(i − r) + k(180° − 2r) and leaves at θ from the antisolar point, the point in the sky straight opposite the Sun (θ = 180° − D for one reflection, D − 180° for two). The ray of least deviation (Descartes 1637) satisfies cos² i = (n² − 1)/(k² + 2k): near it many rays leave at almost the same angle, and that pile up is the bow. The share of light on each ray is from the Fresnel equations for light polarized across (s) and along (p) the plane of the ray, (1 − R)² Rᵏ, with R the same at every surface of the ray (Stokes relations); sunlight is unpolarized, an even mix. The sky picture adds, at each angle from the antisolar point, the light of every ray from an evenly lit drop (12 000 rays per wavelength, 400 to 700 nm every 5 nm; light reflected off the outside, one reflection and two reflections inside), divided by the solid angle it spreads into, and averages it over the Sun's disk, 0.53° across. Sunlight is a blackbody at 5772 K (the Sun's effective temperature). Colors use the CIE 1931 color matching functions (fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013) and sRGB; colors outside the screen's range are desaturated, so the bow looks paler than in life. Left out: diffraction (Airy theory), which makes the real bow slightly wider and adds the faint supernumerary bows inside the primary; drops flattened by the air (large drops); light scattered twice; the sky's own light. The cloud behind and the hills are drawn, not computed. The sky picture is a map of azimuth across and elevation up, like a panorama photograph, so circles look slightly wider near the top. In 3D the shower is a curtain of rain 30 to 33 m in front of you, its drops drawn far larger than life (and larger still where they send you more light), falling at 4 m/s, about the terminal speed of a 1 mm drop (Gunn and Kinzer 1949); each drop's color is the light it sends to the observer's eye.
