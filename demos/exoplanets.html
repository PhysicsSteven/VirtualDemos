More about this

Light molecules make big features. In transit, starlight passes through the ring of air at the planet's edge. Where a molecule absorbs, the air is opaque higher up, so the planet looks bigger at that wavelength. The height scale is H = kT/(μ mu g): hot air, low gravity and light molecules give a tall, puffy atmosphere. Hydrogen and helium (μ = 2.33) give features 18.9 times bigger than carbon dioxide (μ = 44.0) at the same T and g. That is how JWST tells a puffy hot Jupiter from a compact rocky world.

Gravity comes from the planet's mass and radius: change the mass to change g without changing the transit depth. Drag across the spectrum, or use the arrow keys on it, to read the depth at one wavelength; the telescope view then draws the atmosphere's ring at that wavelength, thickened as it says.

Model: z(λ) = H ln(σ(λ)/σref), the altitude where a grazing ray becomes opaque in an isothermal atmosphere (Lecavelier des Etangs et al. 2008; de Wit and Seager 2013), and depth ((Rp + z)/Rs)². The bands sit at their real wavelengths (Na 589 nm; H₂O 1.4, 1.9, 2.7 and 6.3 µm; CH₄ 3.3 µm; CO₂ 4.3 and 15 µm; and others), but their shapes are schematic: each is a smooth bump, and the strongest band of each gas is 2 scale heights tall, the typical n for a clear atmosphere (Kreidberg 2018). So the heights scale exactly as H, but the detailed shapes are not real cross sections. The continuum is Rayleigh scattering, whose slope is exact where it dominates (the radius falls by 4H for each factor of e in wavelength), on a flat floor standing for weaker absorption. Rp from the slider is the radius in the windows between bands, the one the light curve uses. In a hydrogen atmosphere methane gives way to carbon monoxide above about 1,000 K (a smooth switch; Lodders and Fegley 2002). Clouds are gray and opaque: nothing below the cloud top is seen, the cloud top becomes the windows' level, and only bands that reach above it show (the cloud top slider is in scale heights above the clear windows). Ignored: temperature changing with height, the change of g with height, refraction, the planet's night side glow, and the star's spots.

More about this

The eclipses give the sizes and the temperatures; the two velocity curves give the masses. The deeper eclipse hides the star with the brighter surface. The times of the four contacts, with the stars' speed, give both radii, and the flat bottom of a total eclipse is the light of the star left in view.

More about this

An orbit is a scale. Kepler's third law with Newton's correction, in astronomers' units, gives the total mass: M₁ + M₂ = a³/P², with a the separation in AU, P in years and M in solar masses. The Earth and Sun check it: a = 1, P = 1, M = 1.

The seesaw splits it. Both stars circle their center of mass, and M₁a₁ = M₂a₂: the heavier star sits closer and moves less. Switch to Visual to watch it, or read it from the velocities: the heavier star has the smaller K.

Mass sets the light. On the main sequence, L ≈ M⁴ in solar units (a fit that holds best from about 0.4 to 2 M☉; heavier stars fall below it, nearer M³·⁵). Press Mass and light above the light curve to see both stars against it. A white dwarf or a giant is far off the line.

The two star model

Visual view: the two stars as a telescope would show them if it could see them apart, each moving on its own ellipse about the center of mass, with the orbit tilted by i. Only the shape is drawn: the angle of the orbit on the sky (its node) is not modeled, so the line of nodes is always horizontal. Dot sizes stand for brightness, not size. Spectroscopic view: the combined spectrum near hydrogen's red line (Hα, 656.28 nm in air, NIST ASD) now, and below it one orbit of earlier spectra. Each star's line is shifted by the Doppler formula λ = λ₀(1 + v/c), exact for v much less than c; each line's depth is that star's share of the light, and the line width (a Gaussian 0.025 nm wide) is schematic. Mass and light: L = R²T⁴ in solar units against the main sequence fit L = M⁴.

Two stars in a two body Kepler orbit, each a sphere of uniform temperature. The light is bolometric (every wavelength counted), so a star's surface brightness goes as T⁴ (Stefan Boltzmann) and its luminosity as R²T⁴. Both stars have the same quadratic limb darkening as the planet systems (u₁ = 0.40, u₂ = 0.26), so in a total eclipse the light left is exactly the uneclipsed star's. Ignored: tidal stretching, reflection of one star's light off the other, gravity darkening, light travel time across the orbit and mass transfer, all of which matter in real close binaries. The velocities are the stars' true line of sight velocities plus γ. Noise is white and Gaussian, up to 1% in brightness and 10 km/s in velocity. Star colors are approximate blackbody colors. A mystery binary draws a star 1 of 1.0 to 2.4 M☉ and a lighter star 2 (0.35 to 0.8 times as heavy), each on a simple main sequence (R ∝ M^0.8, L ∝ M⁴, so T = 5772 K × M^0.6), a circular orbit with a period of 1.5 to 5 days, an inclination at which both eclipses are total or annular, and γ between −40 and +40 km/s; only T₁ is given, as a spectrum would give it.

The model and its formulas

Model: one planet and its star in a two body orbit. The planet's own light is ignored, so there is no secondary eclipse. Limb darkening is quadratic with fixed Sun like coefficients (u₁ = 0.40, u₂ = 0.26): the middle of the star is brighter than its average, so at the middle of a central transit the dip is 1/(1 − u₁/3 − u₂/6) = 1.215 times (Rp/Rs)² for a small planet (1.210 times when Rp/Rs = 0.2). The duration readout uses the standard approximation for eccentric orbits. Noise is white and Gaussian. With noise, the readouts and Record reading give the measurement nearest the cursor (brightness from the closely spaced set inside the transit window, else from the whole orbit set) and the time of that measurement; without noise they give the true curves at the cursor. A mystery system draws a star of 0.70 to 1.30 M☉ (radius M^0.8) and a rocky planet, a mini Neptune or a giant, with a density in that kind's range, a circular orbit 0.03 to 0.10 AU out, and an impact parameter below 0.5. In the top view the star and planet are drawn larger than scale.
