Diffraction Grating

Shine a laser, a gas lamp or white light through a grating. Bright orders land on the wall where d sin θ = mλ: measure them to find the wavelength.

More about this

Whole numbers of d/a make orders go missing: the zero of one slit's pattern lands on a bright order. Turning the grating uses d(sin θm − sin θi) = mλ, angles from the grating's normal.

Single slit swaps the grating for one slit of width a (N = 1): the pattern is [sin β/β]² alone, with dark fringes where a sin θ = mλ. The slits here are a few micrometers wide so the pattern spreads across the wall; a slit ten times wider gives the same pattern ten times smaller.

Drag the caliper jaws A and B on the wall onto two spots (m = 0 and m = 1). Then enter L, y and m, and compute. The light meter rides on jaw B: a small detector that reads the intensity there as a fraction of the center's.

More about this

Zoom the plot with a few slits (try N = 5): between two bright orders there are N − 1 zeros and N − 2 small maxima. With hundreds of slits the orders become sharp spikes.

Model

Positions are exact: sin θm = sin θi + mλ/d and ym = L tan θm on a flat wall square to the beam. The plot is the far field pattern of N slits of width a: I ∝ [sin(Nφ)/sin φ]² × [sin β/β]² with φ = πd(sin θ − sin θi)/λ and β = πa(sin θ − sin θi)/λ, scaled so the central peak is 1.

On the wall each order carries power in proportion to the slit envelope; obliquity factors are left out. A spot is as wide as the beam plus its diffraction spread, but never drawn smaller than a few pixels, so its brightness is not to scale. The laser, lamps and grating holder are not to scale; L, y and the angles are.

Single slit: I = I₀[sin β/β]², β = πa(sin θ − sin θi)/λ, the same far field formula with N = 1, and dark fringes at a(sin θ − sin θi) = mλ. The far field needs L much larger than a²/λ, under 1 mm for every slit here, so it holds at every L on the bench. For a slit only a few wavelengths wide this scalar formula is itself an approximation (it leaves out the polarization of the light).

Lamp light passes a slit and a lens to make a parallel beam (a real lamp is too faint to project; in lab you look through the grating). Line data: NIST Handbook of Basic Atomic Spectroscopic Data (J. E. Sansonetti and W. C. Martin), strong neutral lines from 380 to 750 nm, air wavelengths; brightness on a log scale so weak lines stay visible. White light: the CIE D65 daylight spectrum (CIE 15:2004) from 400 to 700 nm. Colors from the CIE 1931 color matching functions (Wyman, Sloan and Shirley fit) in sRGB.
