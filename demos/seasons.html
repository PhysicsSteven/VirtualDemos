Reasons for Seasons

Seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, not from its distance to the Sun. Earth is closest to the Sun in January. The same orbit and tilt make a solar day longer than one turn against the stars, and make a sundial run ahead of or behind a clock.

The model and its formulas

1/P_solar = |1/P_sidereal − 1/P_orbit| (spin backward: P_sidereal < 0) solar days in a year = sidereal days − 1

Spin and orbit: the planet spins at a steady rate and moves at a steady rate on a circular orbit, both counterclockwise seen from above the north pole (backward spin turns clockwise). The marked spot is on the equator; it faces the distant star once every sidereal day and the Sun once every solar day. The star is so far away that the line to it keeps the same direction all year. Sizes and distances in the picture are not to scale. Earth: the stellar day, one turn against the stars, is 86 164.0989 s (IERS); one turn against the March equinox, the sidereal day of star charts, is 8.4 ms shorter because the equinox slowly moves (precession), too small to show here. The year is the sidereal year, 365.256 36 days. The mean solar day is 86 400 s; today's Earth day is about 1 ms longer from tidal slowing, left out. Other planets: sidereal rotation periods, orbital periods and lengths of day from the NASA Planetary Fact Sheets (D. R. Williams, NSSDCA): Mercury 58.6462 d, Venus 243.025 d backward, Mars 24.6229 h, Jupiter 9.9250 h (System III); the Moon's turn is its sidereal month, 27.321 661 d, and its year is Earth's.

EoT = L − α α = atan2(cos ε sin λ, cos λ) M − e sin E... solved exactly solar day = 86 400 s − ΔEoT per day

Through the year: the true Sun against a mean Sun that moves along the equator at a steady rate. L is the mean longitude (280.460° + 0.985 647 4° a day from J2000), M the mean anomaly (357.528° + 0.985 600 3° a day), from the low precision solar coordinates of the Astronomical Almanac, accurate to about 0.01° from 1950 to 2050. The true longitude λ comes from Kepler's equation solved exactly for the eccentricity set, the right ascension α from the tilt ε. Dates are 2026 at noon UT; nutation is left out (a few seconds at most). The equation of time is how far the sundial (apparent solar time) is ahead of the clock (mean solar time); the length of the solar day is the time from one noon on a sundial to the next, which is 86 400 s minus the change in the equation of time over that day. Eccentricity alone uses ε = 0; tilt alone uses e = 0.

Distance or tilt?

— Tilt here
—

Yearly swing in daily insolation, as ± percent of the middle value. The distance swing comes from 1/r², the tilt swing from the chart at constant 1 AU.

More about this

Earth is closest to the Sun around January 3, in Northern winter. Distance also changes both hemispheres together, so it cannot make their seasons opposite.
