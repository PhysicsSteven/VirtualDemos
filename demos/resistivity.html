Resistivity of a Wire

Clip onto a length of wire, drive a current through it and read the voltage across it. R = V/I grows in proportion to the length and falls with the cross section: R = ρL/A, where the resistivity ρ belongs to the metal and rises with temperature.

The magnified wire and the potential

Above the wire is a 2.5 mm piece of it, magnified 200 times and shown in real time. Each dot stands for 10¹⁷ free electrons, spread evenly through the metal, so a wire with four times the area holds four times as many dots along the same length. The rings on its cut end are the four gauges, to the same scale. The dots move at the drift speed v_d = I/(nqA): a thick wire carries the same current with more electrons, each moving more slowly, so the dots cross the ring at I/(e × 10¹⁷) per second whatever the gauge (to within about 1%, since the dots come in whole numbers). Real electrons also move at random, much faster; that motion carries no net current and is left out here (see Inside the wire).

The graph is the potential along the wire, measured from its far end. A steady current makes it fall evenly, by IρL/A over any length L, so the slope is the field E = ρI/A: steeper in a thin wire or a high resistivity alloy. The voltmeter reads the drop between its two clips. Its scale is the next 1, 2 or 5 × 10ⁿ volts; the dashed lines are the other three gauges at the same current, cut off where they leave the scale.

Inside the wire

The model and its formulas

Choose Inside the wire above the picture to see the free electrons in a magnified piece of this wire. With Follow the marked electron, the view travels with the circled electron, so the ions stream past it: its zigzag is the random motion, and its slow creep to the left is the drift.

I = nqAv_d · J = I/A = nqv_d · transit time = L/v_d

Model: free electrons in a metal (OpenStax 9.2). The drift speed v_d = I/(nqA) uses the free electron density n above; n does not change with temperature here (thermal expansion changes it by under 0.3%). The random speed is the Fermi speed v_F = ħ(3π²n)^⅓/m of the free electron model, and the mean time between collisions is τ = m/(ne²ρ), so it follows ρ(T). Electrons move in a 3D random direction and are drawn projected onto the page; ions are drawn on a square grid at the mean atomic spacing (copper's real lattice is face centered cubic). The region repeats at its edges, standing for a piece deep inside the wire. The counter counts electrons carried across the dashed plane by the drift; random motion carries electrons across both ways equally on average and is left out of the count. The electrons sit at random positions, so the count scatters about n v_d t × (2 nm × 0.3 nm) like any count; after the drift has carried the electrons one full region width (4 nm), every one has crossed exactly once.

Current in a sheet

The model and its formulas

J = E/R_s · ∇·J = 0 · I = ∮ J·n dl · R = V/I

A thin resistive sheet, like the conducting paper of a field mapping lab, 24 cm by 16 cm, with two electrodes held at the battery's V and at 0 V. In a sheet the current per unit width is J = E/R_s, where the sheet resistance R_s = ρ/t is the resistivity over the thickness (ohms per square: any square of the sheet, whatever its size, has R_s between two opposite edges). In a steady state no charge piles up anywhere, so as much current leaves every bit of sheet as enters it; no current crosses the sheet's edges or a cut. The demo solves that exactly on a grid of 0.25 cm squares (Kirchhoff's rule at every square, conjugate gradients to 10⁻¹⁰ of V), so the map is the true answer for a sheet made of those squares; for two full width bars it gives R = R_s L/W exactly. Silver paint is modeled as 10⁴ times as conductive as the sheet. The tint and the lines every V/10 are equipotentials; the arrows are lines of current, one for each equal share of the current, so they crowd where J is large; they cross the equipotentials at right angles, and they are not drawn inside silver paint, which carries the current along with almost no field. R_s is a setting, not a measured paper. Press the sheet with Probe to read the potential there (the arrow keys move the probe 2.5 mm, Shift 1 cm); with Cut, Silver paint or Mend, drag across the sheet.

Cold stage

The model and its formulas

Choose Cold stage above the picture: a sample wire in a liquid helium cryostat, cooled from room temperature to 1 K while the four wire meters read it. The length and gauge are the ones set above; the current is the supply current.

Model: normal metals follow Matthiessen's rule, ρ(T) = ρ₀ + ρ_ph(T). The lattice part ρ_ph uses the Bloch–Grüneisen formula with the Debye temperature Θ (Kittel, Table 5.1: Cu 343 K, Al 428 K, Sn 200 K, Pb 105 K), scaled so that ρ(293 K) is the handbook value (Cu 1.68, Al 2.65, Sn 11.5, Pb 20.8 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m); real curves differ from this one formula by up to tens of percent between about 10 K and 100 K. The residual part ρ₀ = ρ(293 K)/RRR comes from impurities and defects and depends on the sample. Below the critical temperature (OpenStax Table 9.3: Al 1.20 K, Sn 3.72 K, Pb 7.19 K, YBa₂Cu₃O₇ 92 K) the resistance is exactly zero; the step is drawn sharp, while real transitions are from a millikelvin to about a kelvin wide. Copper does not become superconducting at any temperature reached so far. YBCO's normal state is drawn as ρ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m × T/300 K, a typical value along its copper oxide planes; it varies from sample to sample. Thermal contraction is left out. The points are simulated readings, noise free.

Lab
The model and its formulas

Blind hides ρ, α, the wire's resistance per meter, and ρ and α from the fits, leaving the meters, the rule and the settings. The Mystery wire always hides its own values. Inside the wire, Blind also hides J, v_d, the transit time, I/e, the expected count and the collision time; the count, the drift so far and the real time stay, so v_d can be measured. In the Wire view, Blind hides the drift speed, the expected dot rate and the slope E of the potential (the Mystery wire hides E too).

Model: R = ρL/A with ρ linear in temperature; the voltmeter is ideal (four wire, so the clips and leads add nothing) and the current is set by a constant current supply. Resistivities are handbook values at 20 °C; real alloys vary by a few percent.
