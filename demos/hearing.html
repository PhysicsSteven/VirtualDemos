Hearing and the Ear

The cochlea sorts sound by frequency: each tone makes a traveling wave on the basilar membrane that peaks at its own place. Loudness is not intensity: the equal loudness contours show how many decibels each frequency needs to sound as loud. Then take your own audiogram with headphones and compare it with the 4 kHz notch that noise leaves.

The model and its formulas

Blind hides the place of the tone on the membrane, the loudness in phons and sones, and the label of each hearing level band. Predict first: where does 4 kHz land? Is a 100 Hz tone at 60 dB louder or quieter than 60 phons?

β = 10 log(I/I₀), I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² · f = 165.4 (10^(2.1 x) − 0.88) Hz

Cochlea: Greenwood's place map for the human cochlea (Greenwood 1990, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 87, 2592), with x the fraction of the basilar membrane's length from the apex and the length taken as 35 mm. The traveling wave is drawn schematically: its amplitude grows slowly toward the tone's place and dies out within about 3 mm past it, and its phase lags more and more on the way, as Békésy observed; the shape is not computed from the mechanics. The wave is slowed so that one cycle takes 1 s on screen, at every frequency. Damaged hair cells are drawn where Greenwood's map puts 3 to 6 kHz.

Loudness: the equal loudness contours of ISO 226:2003 (pure tones, free field, frontal incidence, listeners 18 to 25 years old with normal hearing), from its Table 1 and equations (1) and (2), for 20 to 90 phons and its threshold of hearing T_f. Between the standard's 29 frequencies its three parameters are interpolated linearly in log f. The standard's two equations are not exact inverses: at 1 kHz they agree with phons equal to decibels within 0.04 dB. Sones: N = 2^((L_N − 40)/10), one sone at 40 phons. OpenStax reads its examples from older curves (Robinson and Dadson 1956), so at low frequencies its answers differ from ISO 226 by several phons.

Audiogram: hearing level in dB HL, 0 dB being the normal threshold at each frequency. The demo cannot calibrate your headphones, so your thresholds are on its own scale (0 dB = 100 dB below full scale) and plotted relative to your threshold at 1 kHz. With "Subtract the normal threshold curve" on, the shape of the ISO 226 threshold relative to 1 kHz is subtracted too, which assumes headphones with a flat response; real headphones are not flat, so read the shape, not the numbers. The search follows a simplified Hughson-Westlake method (down 10 dB, up 5 dB, two responses on the way up). The preset audiograms are illustrative, shaped like the three in OpenStax College Physics 2e, Figure 17.37: not measurements. Bands of hearing level: Clark 1981 (ASHA 23, 493): normal up to 15 dB HL, slight 16 to 25, mild 26 to 40, moderate 41 to 55, moderately severe 56 to 70, severe 71 to 90, profound above 90.

Sound: Web Audio sine tones with 20 ms rises and falls, sent to one ear in the test. The demo never sets a level above 20 dB below digital full scale.
