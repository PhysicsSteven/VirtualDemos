Timbre and Harmonics

A steady musical tone is a sum of harmonics at whole number multiples of its fundamental. Shape the spectrum bar by bar and hear it: the mix of harmonics sets the tone color, the repetition period sets the pitch, and the phases barely matter.

Each harmonic is aₙ sin(2π fₙ t + φₙ); a dial points along φₙ, measured counterclockwise from the right. Randomizing the phases reshapes the waveform and its peak but leaves every amplitude, the spectrum and the RMS unchanged. A steady tone sounds almost the same: the ear is nearly deaf to phase (Ohm's acoustic law). It is not fully deaf: at a low f₁ (try 55 Hz) several high harmonics share one filter in the cochlea, their phases shape the buzz you hear there, and sharp attacks and clicks depend on phase too.

Inharmonic partials

Shape cycles every
—
Partial 16 / 16 f₁
—

Detune one harmonic and the waveform stops repeating: its shape drifts through a full cycle every 1/|Δf|, and you hear a slow, regular change at |Δf| per second. Mistuned by more than a few percent, the harmonic starts to stand out as a separate tone. Stiffness stretches every partial upward, fₙ = n f₁ √((1 + Bn²)/(1 + B)), as in a stiff string; piano strings have a small B, one reason pianos are tuned with slightly wide octaves. A large B gives a clangy, bell-like tone without one clear pitch. Real bells have their own particular partials; this only shows what inharmonic partials do.
