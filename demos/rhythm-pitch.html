From Rhythm to Pitch

Turn a toothed wheel slowly and a card clicks on every tooth: a beat you can count. Spin it faster and the clicks run together into a note. Put more wheels on the same axle and their teeth set a ratio: 4 : 5 : 6 is a rhythm when the axle turns slowly and a major chord when it spins fast. Change the axle's speed bar by bar and the chords make a tune.

    How a beat becomes a note

    A wheel with n teeth turning Ω times a second flicks its card n·Ω times a second. Every flick is the same short click, so the sound repeats every 1/(nΩ) seconds.

    Any sound that repeats r times a second is a sum of sine waves at r, 2r, 3r and so on (Fourier's theorem). Below about 10 hits a second you hear each click and can count them: rhythm. Above about 30 a second the ear can no longer separate them and hears the repetition rate as a pitch: r hits a second is a note of r hertz. In between, the clicks blur into a flutter. Nothing changes in the air at 20 or 30 hits a second; only the way the ear reports it changes.

    All the wheels share one axle, so their hit rates keep the ratio of their teeth at every speed. 4 : 5 : 6 is a polyrhythm when the axle is slow and a major chord when it is fast. The whole pattern repeats once every turn, Ω times a second.

    Why small ratios sound smooth: the harmonics of 4Ω and 5Ω meet at 20Ω, 40Ω and so on. Turn on Harmonics of each wheel: a dot marks every harmonic two wheels share. 15 : 16 shares almost none that you can hear, and its neighboring harmonics beat against each other: a rough semitone.

    A piano is tuned in equal temperament: every semitone is the same ratio, 2^(1/12), 100 cents. Its major third is 400 cents; the 5 : 4 of two wheels is 386.3 cents, 14 cents narrower.

    Félix Savart built toothed wheels like these around 1830 to study the limits of hearing.

    The model and its formulas

    hit rate r = n Ω · one click: e^(−t/τ) sin(2π f t), τ = 1 ms · harmonics at m r, m = 1, 2, 3, …

    Model: each tooth flicks its card once as it passes the top, so a wheel's clicks are exactly evenly spaced. A click is a short damped ring (τ = 1 ms); each wheel's card rings at its own frequency, 900 to 3,000 Hz, so the wheels sound different as a rhythm. Above 40 hits a second each click is played quieter, as 1/√(rate), so the loudness of the note stays the same as the axle speeds up (with equal clicks, 1,000 hits a second would be 14 dB louder than 40). The motor reaches a new speed in about 0.1 s; in the Song it changes speed at the start of each bar. Notes are named for equal temperament with A4 = 440 Hz. The edges of rhythm (about 10 hits a second: London, Hearing in Time, 2012) and of pitch (about 30: Pressnitzer, Patterson and Krumbholz, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 109, 2074, 2001) differ from person to person and with the sound; they are marked as bands, not lines.
