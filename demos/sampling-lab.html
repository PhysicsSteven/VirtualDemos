Sampling Lab

A lecture hall of 400 students, every one shown. Take samples of 20 five different ways and see who gets picked, run a study where students choose for themselves against one where a coin decides, then draw thousands of samples and watch the means pile up into a normal curve whose spread shrinks as 1/√n.

How it works

Everything here is simulated: no real students were measured. Each population is 400 values drawn once from a seeded random number generator: pocket change (30 % of students carry none; the rest carry 1 + a Poisson count with mean 2.5 of coins, each a penny, nickel, dime or quarter), reaction times (150 ms plus an exponential wait with mean 90 ms), seconds past the minute (uniform from 0 to 60) and commute times (45 % walkers near 28 min, 55 % drivers near 11 min). The values are then seated so that larger values tend to sit toward the back and near the door, as real habits might, which is what makes some methods biased.

Sampling methods take 20 students without replacement. Simple random: any 20. Stratified: 5 at random from each of the four sections. Cluster: two of the forty blocks of 10 seats, everyone in them. Systematic: a random start among the first 20 seats, then every 20th seat counting row by row. Convenience: 20 of the 50 students nearest the door. Each method has its own stream of random numbers, so its 100th sample is the same whatever you drew before.

The study: each student's weekly study hours and the score they would get without the videos (50 + 3 points an hour + a normal scatter with σ = 6) are simulated once. The true effect is added to every treated score. Self selected: a student with h hours joins with probability 1/(1 + e^(−(h − 6)/1.5)). Randomized: a shuffle puts exactly 200 in each group.

Central limit theorem: each sample draws n students with replacement (a student can be drawn twice), so the draws are independent and the standard deviation of the sample mean is exactly σ/√n, with σ the population's standard deviation (divided by N = 400). The normal curve drawn has mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n (or nμ and √n σ for sums). The skew of the means is the sample skewness of the means drawn; the theorem predicts the population's skewness divided by √n.
