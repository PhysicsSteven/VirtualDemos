Sound Intensity and Decibels

A loudspeaker in an open field spreads its power P over a sphere, so the intensity falls as I = P/(4πr²). The sound level meter reads β = 10 log(I/I₀) in decibels: every doubling of the distance takes off 6.0 dB. Add a second source and find out why two 80 dB sources make 83 dB, not 160.

I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m², free field

More about this

Each reading stores r, the sources and the meter's level. The graph puts r on a doubling scale (log₂ r), so the inverse square law is a straight line; its slope is the change in level per doubling. It uses the readings taken with the sources as they are set now.

Model

Each source is a point that sends its power P out equally in all directions, high above an open field with nothing to reflect the sound (a free field), so I = P/(4πr²) exactly and no energy is lost in the air. (Air absorbs about 5 dB per km at 1 kHz, 20 °C and 50 % humidity, by ISO 9613-1: about 0.15 dB over 32 m, left out.) Close to a real loudspeaker, within about its own size, the point source picture fails; the demo starts at 0.5 m. Source B stands beside A, at the same distance from the meter.

The sources play independent noise, so their intensities add: I = I_A + I_B + I_background, and β = 10 log(I/I₀). Two loudspeakers playing the same tone would interfere instead (OpenStax section 16.5, Interference of Waves). The background is a steady noise of the chosen level, the same everywhere. The meter shows the level to 0.1 dB, as a real meter does; the pressure amplitude uses ρ = 1.20 kg/m³ and v = 343 m/s.
